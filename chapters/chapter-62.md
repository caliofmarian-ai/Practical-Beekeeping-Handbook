# Chapter 62 — Marketing

## Introduction

Marketing is the system used to understand customers, shape products for real demand, communicate truthful value, choose sales channels, and build repeat purchasing. Advertising is only one part of that system.

A beekeeper can produce excellent honey and still build a weak business if customers do not understand why the product is different, cannot find it easily, do not trust the claims, or never return for a second purchase. The opposite problem is equally dangerous: aggressive marketing can create demand that the operation cannot supply consistently or can encourage claims that exceed the evidence or the law.

Honey marketing has an unusual foundation: provenance matters. Customers often care about where honey was harvested, which floral source dominates, who produced it, how it tastes, and how it differs from anonymous blends. These qualities can create value, but only when they are supported by traceability and honest product descriptions.

Current EU rules make this especially important. From 14 June 2026, blends of honey marketed in the EU generally must identify the countries of origin in descending order by weight and include each country's percentage contribution, subject to specified rules. Floral or vegetable-origin descriptions are allowed only when the honey comes wholly or mainly from the stated source and has the corresponding organoleptic, physicochemical, and microscopic characteristics. In Ireland, current FSAI guidance also states that there are no authorised health claims for honey; non-authorised claims cannot be used on labels, packaging, websites, menus, or advertising, and food information must not attribute disease-preventing, treating, or curing properties to honey.

Marketing therefore begins with evidence. A credible story is more valuable than an exaggerated one.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- distinguish marketing, advertising, sales, branding, and promotion;
- identify useful customer segments from actual buying behaviour;
- define a clear value proposition without unsupported medical or environmental claims;
- position honey and other hive products by origin, sensory quality, convenience, format, service, and trust;
- design a product portfolio and pack-size strategy;
- compare direct retail, farmers markets, online, wholesale, food-service, gift, and B2B channels;
- price and promote products without undermining margin or customer trust;
- support floral, geographical, local, organic, raw, and sustainability claims with appropriate evidence;
- understand current EU honey-origin and health-claim constraints as jurisdiction-specific examples;
- build repeat purchasing through customer service, email, content, sampling, and reliable fulfilment;
- measure conversion, average order value, customer acquisition cost, repeat rate, channel margin, stock turn, and sell-through;
- create a practical marketing plan tied to inventory and production capacity.

## 62.1 Marketing Is More Than Promotion

Marketing includes:

- customer research;
- product design;
- positioning;
- pricing;
- packaging;
- distribution;
- communication;
- sales support;
- customer retention;
- measurement.

Promotion is only the communication layer.

## 62.2 Sales and Marketing Are Different

Marketing creates and communicates an offer that customers want.

Sales converts specific prospects into transactions.

A market stall conversation is sales. Choosing which jar sizes to bring, what prices to use, and which customer group the stall serves is marketing.

## 62.3 Branding Is Also Different

Branding creates recognisable identity and meaning around the business.

Chapter 63 covers branding in detail.

Marketing uses that brand in the wider system of product, price, place, promotion, and customer experience.

## 62.4 Start With Customer Behaviour

A useful segment is defined by meaningful differences in buying behaviour or needs.

Examples can include:

- local households buying everyday honey;
- gift buyers;
- premium varietal-honey enthusiasts;
- restaurants/cafés;
- independent food retailers;
- tourists;
- online repeat customers;
- beekeepers buying queens/nuclei;
- growers buying pollination services.

Do not define segments only by vague demographic stereotypes.

## 62.5 Customer Problem or Desire

Ask what the customer is actually seeking.

Possible needs include:

- dependable everyday sweetener;
- local provenance;
- distinctive flavour;
- premium gift;
- traceable origin;
- convenient squeeze packaging;
- reliable wholesale supply;
- documented colony strength for pollination;
- locally adapted queen genetics.

Marketing should connect the real product to the real need.

## 62.6 Customer Interviews

Useful questions include:

- Which honey do you currently buy?
- Where do you buy it?
- What makes you switch brands?
- Which jar size do you finish?
- What makes a product feel premium?
- Which information on the label matters?
- What price difference would change your choice?

Avoid leading questions such as “Would you buy my amazing local honey?”

## 62.7 Observe Purchases, Not Compliments

People may praise a product and still not buy it.

Stronger evidence includes:

- actual purchase;
- repeat purchase;
- pre-order;
- email signup followed by purchase;
- wholesale reorder.

## 62.8 Market Size

Estimate demand through:

- local population;
- retail outlets;
- market footfall;
- current category prices;
- competitor stock movement;
- online search and sales data where available;
- own test sales.

Avoid multiplying population by an unrealistic assumed annual consumption.

## 62.9 Competitor Analysis

Study comparable products for:

- size;
- price;
- origin;
- packaging;
- channel;
- claims;
- reviews;
- visual presentation;
- availability.

The goal is not to copy. It is to understand the choices customers already have.

## 62.10 Direct Competitors

Direct competitors sell a similar product to a similar customer.

Examples:

- another local beekeeper selling 340 g jars at the same market;
- a premium Irish varietal honey online.

## 62.11 Indirect Competitors

Indirect competitors solve the same customer need differently.

For a breakfast sweetener, competition can include:

- jam;
- maple syrup;
- sugar spreads;
- other sweeteners.

For a gift, competition can include chocolates, wine, preserves, or candles.

## 62.12 Value Proposition

A value proposition explains why a customer should choose the product.

Strong examples are specific:

- single-apiary seasonal honey with documented origin;
- small-batch comb honey from fresh broodless comb;
- dependable wholesale case supply with full traceability;
- locally adapted overwintered nuclei with health records.

Weak propositions use empty words such as “premium quality” without evidence.

## 62.13 Differentiation

Differentiation can come from:

- verified local origin;
- floral origin;
- sensory profile;
- packaging format;
- customer service;
- availability;
- story/provenance;
- certification;
- traceability;
- specialist product expertise.

## 62.14 Differentiation Must Be Defensible

Do not claim:

- “chemical-free” when no evidence supports it;
- “organic” without certification where certification is required;
- “raw” as a medical benefit;
- “local” when product is blended with imported honey;
- a monofloral origin without supporting evidence.

## 62.15 Product Positioning

Positioning is the place the product aims to occupy in the customer's mind.

Possible positions include:

- everyday local honey;
- premium gift honey;
- rare varietal honey;
- sustainable refill product;
- specialist comb honey;
- dependable B2B supply.

Trying to occupy every position at once creates confusion.

## 62.16 Product Portfolio

A portfolio can include:

- standard liquid honey;
- creamed honey;
- comb honey;
- limited seasonal varietal honey;
- wax products;
- gift sets.

Keep the range small enough to manage inventory and labels efficiently.

## 62.17 Too Many SKUs

Every SKU can require:

- separate stock;
- label;
- packaging;
- price;
- photograph;
- website listing;
- sales history.

A large product range can hide low-selling inventory.

## 62.18 Pack Size Strategy

Different pack sizes serve different needs.

Small packs can support:

- gifts;
- tasting;
- premium trial;
- travel.

Large packs can support:

- households;
- value buyers;
- food-service.

## 62.19 Price Architecture

A range can use price steps that make differences clear.

For example:

- standard local honey;
- limited varietal honey;
- comb honey/gift product.

Do not create artificial tiers with identical product and unsupported claims.

## 62.20 Cost Sets a Floor, Value Influences the Ceiling

Chapter 60 covers unit economics.

Marketing adds the customer's willingness to pay.

A price below sustainable cost is not a marketing strategy; it is a loss.

## 62.21 Premium Pricing

Premium pricing requires reasons customers can understand and trust.

Possible supporting factors include:

- scarce floral crop;
- exceptional sensory profile;
- verified origin;
- difficult comb-honey production;
- certified product;
- high-quality gift packaging.

## 62.22 Discounting

Discounts can increase trial or clear stock, but frequent discounts can teach customers to wait.

Track whether the discount increases:

- new customers;
- repeat purchase;
- total margin;

not only unit sales.

## 62.23 Bundles

Bundles can combine:

- several flavours/origins;
- honey and wax candle;
- gift packaging;
- small tasting jars.

Bundle pricing should preserve margin and truthful product identity.

## 62.24 Direct Retail

Advantages:

- highest control over presentation;
- direct feedback;
- full customer relationship;
- potentially higher gross margin.

Costs include:

- selling time;
- retail systems;
- payment fees;
- customer service.

## 62.25 Wholesale

Wholesale customers may include:

- independent grocers;
- farm shops;
- speciality food stores;
- hotels;
- cafés;
- gift shops.

They need reliable margins, case sizes, lead times, and supply.

## 62.26 Retailer Margin

A retailer must earn a margin too.

If the producer's retail price leaves no room for a retailer, wholesale expansion becomes difficult.

Design channel pricing before approaching stores.

## 62.27 Wholesale Sell Sheet

A simple sell sheet can include:

- product image;
- pack size;
- case quantity;
- wholesale price;
- suggested retail price where appropriate;
- origin;
- shelf life/best-before approach;
- lead time;
- ordering contact;
- legal product information.

## 62.28 Samples for Buyers

Samples should represent the product actually supplied.

Do not send a premium seasonal sample and later substitute a different blend without agreement.

Maintain sample lot identity.

## 62.29 Food-Service Customers

Restaurants and cafés may value:

- larger packs;
- predictable supply;
- invoices;
- consistent flavour;
- delivery reliability.

Retail-style gift packaging may be irrelevant to them.

## 62.30 Farmers Markets

Markets combine sales and research.

They allow observation of:

- questions customers ask;
- pack sizes picked up;
- tasting response;
- price resistance;
- repeat customers.

Chapter 65 covers market operations in detail.

## 62.31 Online Channels

Online can include:

- own website;
- marketplace;
- social-commerce tool;
- subscription/repeat-order system.

Chapter 64 covers e-commerce operations and legal requirements.

## 62.32 Distance-Selling Food Information

Under current EU food-information rules, mandatory food information for prepacked food offered by distance selling—except the date of minimum durability/use-by particular—must generally be available before the purchase is concluded, and all mandatory particulars must be available at delivery.

This affects product-page design, not only the physical jar label.

## 62.33 Local Delivery

Local delivery can strengthen direct sales but has real cost.

Track:

- kilometres;
- driver time;
- failed delivery;
- packaging;
- minimum order.

Free delivery can destroy margin on small orders.

## 62.34 Subscription or Repeat Orders

Honey is naturally suited to repeat purchasing when consumption is regular.

A repeat-order programme can improve:

- forecast demand;
- customer lifetime value;
- retention.

Do not overcomplicate the subscription before demand exists.

## 62.35 Seasonal Demand

Sales often increase around:

- gifting periods;
- tourism season;
- winter food shopping;
- local events.

Build inventory and packaging plans before peak demand.

## 62.36 Gift Products

Gift buyers value:

- attractive presentation;
- clear origin;
- story;
- easy choice;
- reliable shipping.

Gift packaging should not create excessive cost or misleading luxury claims.

## 62.37 Sampling and Tastings

Tasting can convert uncertain buyers.

Use food-safe sampling practices:

- clean utensils;
- single-use portions where appropriate;
- allergen/ingredient information;
- protected food;
- hand hygiene.

## 62.38 Sensory Vocabulary

Useful descriptions are specific and non-medical:

- floral;
- malty;
- caramel-like;
- herbal;
- citrus-like;
- mild;
- robust;
- creamy;
- fine-crystallised.

Avoid inventing medicinal effects from flavour.

## 62.39 Storytelling

A strong product story can include:

- apiary location;
- flowering season;
- beekeeper process;
- harvest conditions;
- traceability;
- family/business history.

Keep every factual statement true and documentable.

## 62.40 Origin Is a Marketing Asset

Country, region, apiary, and floral source can create value.

But the stronger the origin claim, the stronger the evidence required.

Traceability converts provenance from a story into a verifiable claim.

## 62.41 Current EU Honey-Origin Rules

For honey blends placed on the EU market under the rules applying from 14 June 2026, countries of origin generally must appear in the principal field of vision, in descending order by weight, together with their percentage share, subject to the Directive's tolerances and limited Member State options.

A marketing team cannot invent origin percentages after packing; processing records must support them.

## 62.42 Floral-Origin Claims

Under the EU honey framework, a floral or vegetable-origin description can be used where the honey comes wholly or mainly from the named source and has the corresponding organoleptic, physicochemical, and microscopic characteristics.

Visual observation of flowers around the apiary alone is insufficient evidence.

## 62.43 Regional-Origin Claims

EU rules permit regional, territorial, or topographical origin information where the honey comes entirely from the indicated origin.

Blended origins require truthful labelling.

## 62.44 “Local” Claims

“Local” can mean different things to customers.

Define what it means operationally:

- harvested within a county;
- harvested within a stated radius;
- packed locally from imported honey;
- produced and packed locally.

Do not use ambiguity to create a false impression.

## 62.45 “Raw” Claims

“Raw honey” is not defined identically in every jurisdiction.

If used, the business should define its processing practice accurately and avoid implying medically superior effects that are not authorised.

## 62.46 Organic Claims

Organic beekeeping has specific legal/certification rules in regulated markets.

Do not market honey as organic merely because no synthetic treatment was used that season.

Chapter 70 covers organic beekeeping.

## 62.47 Sustainability Claims

Claims such as:

- sustainable;
- bee-friendly;
- regenerative;
- plastic-free;
- carbon-neutral

need clear definitions and evidence.

Avoid vague environmental marketing that cannot be substantiated.

## 62.48 Health Claims

Food health claims are regulated.

In Ireland/EU, current FSAI guidance states that there are no authorised health claims for honey.

Non-authorised claims cannot be used in commercial communications including labels, websites, menus, or advertising.

## 62.49 Disease Claims

Food information must not claim or imply that honey prevents, treats, or cures human disease.

Do not market honey as a treatment for:

- infection;
- cancer;
- allergy;
- diabetes;
- wounds

unless the product has an entirely different authorised regulatory status that permits the claim.

## 62.50 Nutrition Claims

Nutrition claims such as “source of” or “high in” nutrients have defined legal conditions in regulated markets.

Natural presence of a nutrient does not automatically permit the claim.

## 62.51 Research References in Advertising

Citing a study does not automatically make an unauthorised claim lawful.

A marketing sentence can imply treatment even if every word is technically linked to a paper.

Review the overall impression to the consumer.

## 62.52 Testimonials

Customer testimonials can create compliance risk if they make unauthorised medical claims.

A business should not republish “this honey cured my condition” as advertising simply because a customer wrote it.

## 62.53 Influencers and Affiliates

Paid or incentivised promotion should be transparently disclosed under applicable advertising law.

Provide promoters with approved factual product information and prohibited-claim guidance.

## 62.54 Photography

Good product photography should show the real product accurately.

Useful images include:

- clear jar front;
- texture;
- comb detail;
- apiary/context;
- scale/pack size.

Do not digitally alter colour so far that the delivered honey looks materially different.

## 62.55 Video

Video can demonstrate:

- extraction;
- creamed-honey texture;
- comb cutting;
- apiary environment;
- packaging.

Protect food hygiene and worker safety while filming.

## 62.56 Content Marketing

Useful educational topics include:

- why honey crystallises;
- how floral sources differ;
- how to store honey;
- comb honey use;
- what traceability means;
- seasonal apiary work.

Education builds trust when it remains accurate.

## 62.57 Search Visibility

Website content should use the words customers actually search for, such as:

- local honey;
- comb honey;
- Irish honey;
- honey gift set;
- varietal honey.

Do not stuff pages with repetitive keywords that reduce readability.

## 62.58 Local Search

Keep business information consistent across relevant listings:

- name;
- address/service area;
- opening hours;
- contact details.

Incorrect information damages trust and wastes visits.

## 62.59 Reviews

Reviews can improve trust.

Ask satisfied customers for genuine reviews but do not:

- fabricate reviews;
- buy fake ratings;
- hide legitimate complaints dishonestly.

## 62.60 Responding to Negative Reviews

Respond with:

- facts;
- courtesy;
- solution where appropriate.

Do not disclose private customer data in a public response.

## 62.61 Email Marketing

Email can support repeat sales through:

- harvest announcements;
- restock alerts;
- market dates;
- educational content;
- gift launches.

Collect and use personal data according to applicable privacy and electronic-marketing rules.

## 62.62 Permission and Privacy

Do not add customers to marketing lists merely because you have their email from an order unless local law permits that use and required conditions are met.

Provide appropriate privacy information and unsubscribe mechanisms.

## 62.63 Customer Relationship Management

Even a simple CRM can track:

- wholesale contacts;
- order history;
- follow-up dates;
- preferences;
- consent status.

Do not collect more personal data than the business needs.

## 62.64 Repeat Rate

Repeat purchase rate indicates whether customers return.

A simple version is:

`repeat customers ÷ total customers in the cohort`

Define the time period consistently.

## 62.65 Average Order Value

`AOV = total order revenue ÷ number of orders`

AOV can improve through:

- bundles;
- larger jars;
- gifts;
- complementary products.

Do not increase AOV by pushing unsuitable products.

## 62.66 Conversion Rate

Conversion rate can mean:

- website purchasers ÷ visitors;
- buyers ÷ market-stall interactions;
- wholesale accounts opened ÷ qualified leads.

Always define the denominator.

## 62.67 Customer Acquisition Cost

A simplified CAC is:

`marketing and sales acquisition cost ÷ new customers acquired`

Include advertising and relevant labour when possible.

## 62.68 Customer Lifetime Value

CLV estimates the long-term gross profit contribution of a customer relationship.

Do not use revenue alone as lifetime value.

## 62.69 Channel Margin

Compare channels after channel-specific costs.

For example, online sales can have high retail price but also:

- advertising;
- payment fees;
- shipping subsidy;
- packaging;
- returns.

## 62.70 Sell-Through Rate

Sell-through compares units sold with units made available during a period.

It helps identify:

- slow SKUs;
- wrong pack sizes;
- overproduction.

## 62.71 Stock Turn

Stock turn measures how quickly inventory sells and is replaced.

Slow inventory ties up cash and can age on the shelf.

## 62.72 Marketing Return

Do not judge marketing by likes alone.

Measure:

- sales;
- gross profit;
- new customers;
- repeat customers;
- qualified wholesale leads.

## 62.73 Attribution Is Imperfect

A customer may see a market stall, social post, review, and email before buying.

Do not expect perfect attribution.

Use practical indicators and controlled tests.

## 62.74 A/B Testing

Where volume permits, test one variable at a time:

- product image;
- headline;
- bundle;
- call to action.

Do not run complex tests on tiny samples and treat random variation as certainty.

## 62.75 Promotion Calendar

Plan promotions around:

- actual stock;
- harvest availability;
- gifting periods;
- market events;
- production capacity.

Do not advertise a product that cannot be fulfilled reliably.

## 62.76 Limited Editions

A genuine limited floral harvest can create urgency.

Use scarcity claims only when true.

Fake “only 5 left” messages damage trust and can breach consumer law.

## 62.77 Pre-Orders

Pre-orders can test demand and improve cash planning.

State clearly:

- expected fulfilment date;
- refund/cancellation terms;
- quantity limits;
- what happens if crop fails.

Biological crops are uncertain; promises should reflect that.

## 62.78 Out-of-Stock Management

Use out-of-stock periods to:

- collect restock interest;
- offer a truthful alternative;
- explain seasonal supply.

Do not quietly substitute a different origin or blend.

## 62.79 Complaint Handling

Complaints provide quality data.

Record:

- lot;
- issue;
- purchase channel;
- customer evidence;
- resolution.

Repeated complaints can reveal a process failure.

## 62.80 Refunds and Replacement

A clear policy can preserve trust.

Investigate food-safety complaints separately from ordinary preference issues.

Do not resell returned food automatically.

## 62.81 Crisis Communication

If there is a recall or labelling problem:

- communicate quickly;
- identify affected lots;
- state what customers should do;
- avoid speculation;
- update when verified facts change.

Trust is protected by accuracy, not defensiveness.

## 62.82 Wholesale Lead Generation

Potential wholesale outreach can include:

- targeted email;
- sample pack;
- trade fair;
- local food network;
- direct visit by appointment.

Research the buyer first.

## 62.83 Qualifying a Wholesale Lead

Ask:

- Does the shop fit the product?
- What volume can it sell?
- Who decides?
- What margin does it require?
- What delivery schedule is needed?
- Are there listing fees?

## 62.84 Retailer Reorders

A first wholesale order is not success if it never reorders.

Track:

- reorder interval;
- units/store/week;
- returned/damaged units;
- payment timing.

## 62.85 B2B Pollination Marketing

Pollination marketing should emphasise:

- colony strength;
- delivery reliability;
- health records;
- communication;
- contract terms.

Do not market pollination based only on hive count.

## 62.86 B2B Queen/Nucleus Marketing

Useful differentiators include:

- queen lineage;
- mating information;
- health status;
- overwintering status;
- pickup/shipping conditions;
- replacement policy.

Avoid unsupported claims of “mite-proof” or guaranteed performance.

## 62.87 Marketing Budget

A budget can include:

- packaging/design;
- photography;
- website;
- advertising;
- market fees;
- samples;
- printed material;
- promotional labour.

## 62.88 Budget as a Percentage of Sales Is Not Universal

A new brand may require more acquisition spending than an established direct-sales business.

Use expected customer economics rather than copying one industry percentage blindly.

## 62.89 90-Day Marketing Test

A practical test can include:

### Month 1 — Foundation

- define target customer;
- refine product/pack sizes;
- create compliant product information;
- establish baseline metrics.

### Month 2 — Channel Tests

- test one direct channel;
- test one repeat-customer channel;
- contact selected wholesale leads.

### Month 3 — Measure

- compare channel margins;
- repeat rate;
- AOV;
- conversion;
- customer feedback;
- unsold stock.

Then keep, change, or stop each activity.

## 62.90 Marketing Decision Table

| Observation | Likely issue | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Many compliments, few purchases | Weak value/price/channel fit | Test offer, price, pack size and target customer |
| High sales, low margin | Pricing/channel-cost problem | Recalculate channel margin before scaling |
| First orders, no repeat | Product/experience mismatch | Investigate quality, use case and follow-up |
| Premium claim cannot be documented | Trust/legal risk | Remove or gather evidence |
| Website visitors abandon product page | Information/trust/price problem | Improve mandatory info, images, proof and checkout clarity |
| One SKU rarely sells | Portfolio complexity | Reduce, reposition or discontinue |
| Wholesale account orders once | Poor store sell-through | Review retailer fit and shelf support |
| Medical claim drives engagement | Compliance/reputation risk | Remove unauthorised claim even if it attracts clicks |

## 62.92 Scientific and Management Notes

### Origin reduces information uncertainty

Traceable origin can function as a trust signal because customers cannot personally verify every invisible characteristic of food. Strong documentation therefore supports marketing as well as compliance.

### Choice architecture matters

Too many similar products can make decisions harder. A small clear range can outperform a confusing large one.

### Repeat behaviour is stronger evidence than stated preference

Customers may say they value local or premium products, but repeated paid purchases reveal actual willingness to pay.

### Marketing and operations are coupled

Successful promotion can become an operational problem when stock, packing, or customer service cannot keep up. Marketing capacity should therefore match fulfilment capacity.

## Key Takeaways

- Marketing is customer research, offer design, communication, channel selection, and retention—not only advertising.
- Paid repeat purchasing is stronger evidence than compliments.
- A good value proposition is specific and supported by real product evidence.
- Origin and floral-source claims can create value only when traceability supports them.
- EU honey-origin rules applying from 14 June 2026 make accurate blend provenance visible to consumers.
- Current Irish/EU guidance does not authorise health claims for honey, and disease-treatment claims are prohibited for ordinary food marketing.
- Direct, wholesale, market, and online channels have different hidden costs and margins.
- Customer acquisition cost, average order value, repeat rate, channel margin, and sell-through are more useful than social-media likes alone.
- Marketing should not create demand beyond safe production and fulfilment capacity.
- Trust compounds when claims, product quality, origin, service, and records all agree.

## Chapter Summary

Beekeeping marketing works best when it begins with real customer behaviour and real product evidence. The business identifies who buys, what problem or desire the product serves, why its origin or sensory qualities matter, and which channel delivers the product at a sustainable margin.

Honey is especially suited to provenance-based marketing, but provenance must be documented. Current EU rules make country percentages in blended honey visible to consumers and impose conditions on floral and regional origin descriptions. Health marketing is even more constrained: ordinary honey cannot be advertised as a disease treatment, and current Irish guidance reports no authorised health claims for honey.

A professional marketing system therefore relies on truthful differentiation—origin, flavour, format, service, traceability, seasonal scarcity, or certified characteristics—rather than exaggerated wellness promises. It also measures performance by commercial outcomes such as conversion, repeat purchase, average order value, customer acquisition cost, channel margin, and retailer reorders.

Marketing succeeds when it strengthens the complete business rather than merely creating attention. The best campaign is one the operation can fulfil reliably, profitably, and honestly.
