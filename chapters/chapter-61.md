# Chapter 61 — Equipment Costs

## Introduction

Equipment costs can make a beekeeping business look either deceptively cheap or unnecessarily expensive, depending on what is counted. A beginner may total the price of a hive, suit, smoker, and extractor and believe the investment is complete. A commercial operator knows that the true equipment system also includes replacement frames, spare boxes, storage, transport, food-grade containers, cleaning tools, scales, maintenance parts, cold-chain equipment for perishable products, labels, work surfaces, and enough surplus capacity to keep operating when something breaks at the busiest time of year.

The purpose of this chapter is not to publish a list of prices that will be obsolete next year. Instead, it teaches a cost model that can be filled with current local quotations in any country and updated annually.

Every purchase should answer four questions:

1. What production problem does the item solve?
2. What capacity does it add?
3. What is its full ownership cost over its useful life?
4. Is the business already producing enough margin to justify it?

The cheapest purchase is not always the lowest-cost choice. Equipment that breaks during harvest, cannot be cleaned properly, contaminates food, requires excessive labour, or does not match the hive system can be more expensive than a higher-quality item.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- distinguish capital expenditure, operating expense, inventory, and working capital;
- build a complete start-up equipment list by business model;
- calculate cost per colony and cost per unit of output;
- estimate useful life and annual depreciation for management purposes;
- compare buying new, buying used, renting, sharing, and outsourcing;
- identify hygiene, disease, compatibility, and safety risks in used equipment;
- size extraction, storage, bottling, cold-chain, and transport equipment to actual capacity needs;
- calculate total cost of ownership rather than purchase price alone;
- include maintenance, repairs, electricity, cleaning, calibration, insurance, interest, and replacement parts;
- use scenario analysis before investing in mechanisation;
- recognise when redundancy is economically justified;
- maintain an equipment register and replacement plan.

## 61.1 Price Lists Become Obsolete

Equipment prices change with:

- inflation;
- currency;
- shipping;
- material costs;
- tax;
- supplier;
- season;
- scale discounts.

The book therefore teaches cost structure and quotation methods instead of pretending one 2026 catalogue price is permanent.

## 61.2 Build the List From the Business Model

A honey-only operation needs different equipment from a business producing queens, pollen, or royal jelly.

Start with the product flow:

**colony → harvest → transport → processing → storage → packing → sale**

List the equipment required at each step.

## 61.3 Core Apiary Equipment

Typical core items include:

- hive bodies;
- floors/bottom boards;
- roofs;
- brood boxes;
- honey supers;
- frames;
- foundation or starter systems;
- queen excluders where used;
- feeders;
- hive stands;
- straps;
- entrance reducers;
- spare components.

## 61.4 Spares Are Part of the System

A business should not operate with exactly one component per hive.

Spare equipment is needed for:

- swarm capture;
- splits;
- damaged boxes;
- unexpected strong flows;
- disease isolation;
- queen failure;
- emergency feeding.

Budgeting only for equipment currently occupied by bees understates working capacity.

## 61.5 Frames and Foundation

Frames are a recurring cost because:

- comb is retired;
- frames break;
- new colonies are created;
- supers expand;
- disease controls can destroy comb.

Track cost separately for:

- frame components;
- foundation;
- wire/pins;
- labour to assemble.

## 61.6 Assembly Labour

A flat-pack box may be cheaper than an assembled one but requires labour and tools.

Include:

- assembly time;
- screws/nails;
- glue;
- paint/protective coating;
- workspace;
- rejects.

## 61.7 Protective Equipment

Budget for:

- suits/jackets;
- veils;
- gloves;
- boots;
- spare PPE for staff/visitors where required;
- washing/replacement.

PPE is not a one-time lifetime purchase.

## 61.8 Hand Tools

Common tools include:

- smokers;
- hive tools;
- frame lifters;
- brushes;
- queen clips/cages;
- grafting tools if relevant;
- torches;
- scales;
- marking equipment.

Small tools are easy to under-budget because they are purchased gradually.

## 61.9 Smoker Costs

A smoker requires:

- purchase;
- replacement bellows/guards;
- fuel;
- fire-safe transport/storage;
- cleaning.

Cheap smokers that fail repeatedly can waste inspection time.

## 61.10 Monitoring Equipment

Health and production monitoring may require:

- Varroa wash cups;
- alcohol/detergent supplies;
- sample jars;
- magnification;
- hive scales;
- temperature sensors;
- records/software.

Monitoring is a production-control cost, not an optional scientific hobby.

## 61.11 Feeding Equipment

Costs can include:

- feeders;
- syrup tanks;
- pumps;
- food-grade buckets;
- measuring devices;
- protective storage from rodents/ants.

Feed handling can become mechanised as colony count grows.

## 61.12 Queen-Rearing Equipment

A queen business can require:

- mating nuclei;
- queen cups;
- cell bars;
- incubators where used;
- cages;
- marking tools;
- transport boxes;
- grafting station;
- record system.

These costs belong to the queen enterprise, not automatically to honey production.

## 61.13 Pollen Equipment

Pollen production may require:

- traps;
- collection drawers;
- sorting screens;
- freezer;
- dryer or freeze-dryer;
- water-activity/moisture testing;
- suitable packaging.

The cold/dry chain can cost more than the trap itself.

## 61.14 Royal-Jelly Equipment

Royal-jelly production may require:

- cell cups/frames;
- grafting tools;
- collection tools/pumps;
- clean chilled containers;
- refrigeration/freezing;
- cold transport;
- specialised laboratory tests.

## 61.15 Propolis Equipment

Propolis production can require:

- traps;
- freezer/chiller;
- grinder;
- screens;
- solvent-compatible extraction equipment where lawful;
- fire-safe solvent storage;
- filtration;
- light-protective containers.

## 61.16 Bee-Venom Equipment

Venom production requires specialised professional collection equipment, electrical inspection, secure collection plates, PPE, controlled recovery, and laboratory analysis.

Do not price only the collector. Safety and testing can dominate the cost.

## 61.17 Honey Harvest Equipment

Harvest may require:

- escapes;
- blowers;
- brushes;
- super covers;
- food-grade trays;
- trolleys;
- lifting aids;
- transport pallets.

## 61.18 Extraction Room

Core equipment can include:

- uncapping tank;
- knives/forks;
- extractor;
- strainers;
- settling tanks;
- pumps;
- hoses;
- valves;
- food-grade buckets;
- scales;
- refractometer;
- warming cabinet/room where justified.

## 61.19 Extractor Size

Do not size the extractor only by hive count.

Consider:

- peak supers per harvest day;
- frame size;
- time available;
- labour;
- viscosity;
- future growth;
- cleaning time.

## 61.20 Throughput Calculation

A simple capacity estimate is:

`frames/hour = frames per load × loads per hour`

Then compare with expected peak frames.

Include:

- loading;
- unloading;
- uncapping;
- turning where relevant;
- cleaning/downtime.

## 61.21 The Extractor Is Not Always the Bottleneck

A large extractor can sit idle if uncapping cannot feed it quickly enough.

Likewise, fast extraction can overwhelm:

- strainers;
- tanks;
- pumps;
- staff.

Design the line as a system.

## 61.22 Manual Versus Motorised

Manual equipment costs less upfront but requires more labour.

Motorised equipment can reduce labour but adds:

- electricity;
- maintenance;
- controls;
- electrical safety;
- replacement motors/gears.

## 61.23 Bottling Equipment

Options range from:

- manual honey gates;
- bench fillers;
- piston/gear pumps;
- semi-automatic fillers;
- complete packing lines.

The correct investment depends on jars per hour and labour cost.

## 61.24 Packaging Equipment

Do not forget:

- scales;
- cap/lid tools;
- label applicators;
- date/lot coding;
- carton sealing;
- shelving;
- pallet wrap.

## 61.25 Storage Tanks

Tank capacity should allow:

- separation of lots;
- settling;
- temporary crop peaks;
- cleaning;
- future packing.

One enormous tank can reduce traceability flexibility.

## 61.26 Container Inventory

Packaging stock ties up cash.

Track:

- jars;
- lids;
- bottles;
- labels;
- cartons;
- inserts;
- tamper seals.

Buy enough for continuity without overstocking obsolete label designs.

## 61.27 Premises Costs

Equipment cost is affected by the building.

A honey room may need:

- washable surfaces;
- sinks;
- potable water;
- drainage;
- lighting;
- ventilation;
- electrical outlets;
- pest control;
- storage.

A cheap extractor in an unsuitable room does not create a compliant process.

## 61.28 Utilities

Budget for:

- electricity;
- water;
- hot water;
- refrigeration;
- dehumidification;
- heating;
- waste disposal.

## 61.29 Vehicle Costs

Transport can be one of the largest commercial costs.

Include:

- purchase/lease;
- depreciation;
- fuel;
- tyres;
- service;
- tax/registration;
- insurance;
- breakdown;
- cleaning;
- trailer.

## 61.30 Load Capacity

Hive bodies and honey are heavy.

Check:

- legal payload;
- axle limits;
- trailer limits;
- tie-down requirements;
- safe load distribution.

Do not plan using vehicle volume only.

## 61.31 Forklifts and Pallet Systems

Larger operations may use:

- forklift;
- loader;
- pallet jack;
- hive pallets.

Add:

- training;
- maintenance;
- charging/fuel;
- inspection;
- storage.

## 61.32 Digital Equipment

Business tools can include:

- laptop/tablet/phone;
- printer;
- label printer;
- barcode scanner;
- inventory software;
- accounting software;
- website/hosting.

## 61.33 Laboratory and Quality Tools

Routine in-house tools can include:

- refractometer;
- thermometers;
- calibrated scales;
- pH/conductivity tools where justified;
- data loggers.

Advanced authenticity/residue testing is usually more economical to outsource.

## 61.34 Calibration

Measuring tools require:

- calibration/check standards;
- records;
- replacement batteries;
- periodic professional calibration where required.

The purchase price is not the full metrology cost.

## 61.35 Capital Expenditure

Capital equipment provides service for more than one season.

Track each asset by:

- purchase date;
- purchase cost;
- useful life;
- expected salvage value;
- maintenance history.

## 61.36 Straight-Line Depreciation

A simple management estimate is:

`annual depreciation = (purchase cost − estimated salvage value) ÷ useful life`

Tax rules may use different allowances; consult current local tax guidance.

## 61.37 Total Cost of Ownership

TCO includes:

- purchase;
- finance interest;
- delivery;
- installation;
- maintenance;
- energy;
- cleaning;
- consumables;
- repairs;
- downtime;
- disposal/resale.

## 61.38 Cheap Equipment Can Be Expensive

A low-cost item can create high TCO through:

- short life;
- poor cleanability;
- slow throughput;
- unavailable spare parts;
- product damage;
- safety problems.

## 61.39 Expensive Equipment Can Also Be Wasteful

A large machine can destroy return on capital if used only a few hours per year.

Calculate utilisation before buying.

## 61.40 Capacity Utilisation

A simple measure is:

`utilisation = actual throughput ÷ practical available capacity`

Very low utilisation suggests overinvestment unless redundancy or future growth justifies it.

## 61.41 Labour-Saving Investment

Estimate annual labour saving:

`hours saved × true hourly labour cost`

Compare against annual ownership cost.

## 61.42 Payback Period

A simple payback estimate is:

`purchase cost ÷ annual net cash benefit`

It ignores time value of money but is useful for quick comparisons.

## 61.43 Net Present Value

Larger investments can be assessed using discounted cash flow/NPV.

This accounts for:

- timing of cash flows;
- required return;
- equipment life.

Use accounting/financial advice for significant borrowing.

## 61.44 Financing

Equipment may be funded through:

- retained earnings;
- loan;
- lease;
- grant;
- cooperative ownership.

Compare full cost and obligations.

## 61.45 Interest

Borrowed equipment has interest cost even when accounting depreciation is separate.

A machine that appears affordable at cash price can become expensive under poor finance terms.

## 61.46 Grants

Grants can reduce capital cost but may require:

- eligible equipment;
- pre-approval;
- quotes;
- records;
- matching funds;
- retention periods.

Do not purchase first and assume reimbursement later.

## 61.47 Buying Used Equipment

Used equipment can reduce capital costs.

Inspect:

- structural condition;
- food-contact surfaces;
- electrical safety;
- rust;
- seals/bearings;
- spare-part availability;
- contamination history.

## 61.48 Used Hive Equipment and Disease

Second-hand hive bodies and comb can carry disease risk.

Old brood comb can transmit:

- AFB spores;
- other pathogens;
- pests;
- residues.

Disease risk can outweigh purchase savings.

## 61.49 Used Food Equipment

Ask what product was previously processed.

Avoid equipment with unknown history involving:

- chemicals;
- non-food oils;
- allergens;
- corrosion;
- unsuitable metals.

## 61.50 Cleaning Cost Before Use

A used tank may need:

- dismantling;
- seals;
- polishing;
- food-grade hoses;
- professional electrical work.

Include refurbishment in purchase cost.

## 61.51 Renting

Renting can be economical for rarely used equipment.

Advantages:

- low capital requirement;
- no long-term storage;
- maintenance may be included.

Disadvantages:

- availability risk;
- transport;
- cleaning verification;
- scheduling.

## 61.52 Shared Honey Houses

Shared facilities can spread capital cost across producers.

Require rules for:

- booking;
- cleaning;
- lot segregation;
- liability;
- breakage;
- traceability.

## 61.53 Outsourcing Extraction

Contract extraction can postpone equipment investment.

Compare:

- price per super/kg;
- transport;
- turnaround;
- lot control;
- quality system;
- risk of mixing.

## 61.54 Outsourcing Packing

A contract packer can provide professional throughput without owning a filling line.

Requirements include:

- written specification;
- supply-chain traceability;
- packaging ownership;
- label approval;
- minimum run size.

## 61.55 Equipment Compatibility

Standardise hive dimensions where practical.

Mixed formats increase:

- spare inventory;
- frame incompatibility;
- extractor complexity;
- transport inefficiency.

## 61.56 Standardisation

Commercial efficiency improves when common items are standardised:

- box size;
- frame type;
- lid;
- pallet;
- jar family;
- labels;
- hoses/fittings.

## 61.57 Spare Parts

Keep critical spares for harvest equipment:

- belts;
- bearings;
- gaskets;
- valves;
- fuses;
- pump seals;
- controller components where user-serviceable.

## 61.58 Downtime Cost

A breakdown during a one-week harvest window can cost far more than the spare part.

Estimate downtime cost through:

- lost honey quality;
- extra labour;
- delayed harvest;
- rental/emergency repair.

## 61.59 Preventive Maintenance

Schedule maintenance before peak season.

Tasks can include:

- extractor inspection;
- pump service;
- vehicle service;
- freezer check;
- scale calibration;
- electrical inspection.

## 61.60 Equipment Register

Record:

- asset ID;
- description;
- supplier;
- serial number;
- purchase date;
- cost;
- location;
- maintenance;
- warranty;
- expected replacement.

## 61.61 Replacement Reserve

Set aside money for future replacement.

If an extractor has a 10-year useful life, the business should earn enough over those years to replace it without crisis borrowing.

## 61.62 Safety Equipment

Budget explicitly for:

- fire extinguisher appropriate to risk;
- first-aid kits;
- eye protection;
- heat gloves;
- guards;
- lifting aids;
- electrical protection.

Safety is part of equipment cost.

## 61.63 Fire Safety

Wax, ethanol, fuel, smokers, and electrical equipment create different fire risks.

Use appropriate professional fire assessment rather than buying one generic extinguisher and assuming every hazard is covered.

## 61.64 Manual Handling

Mechanisation may be justified by injury reduction even when direct labour payback is modest.

Consider:

- trolleys;
- lifts;
- smaller supers;
- pallet systems.

## 61.65 Cold-Chain Equipment

Perishable hive products may require:

- refrigerator;
- freezer;
- temperature data logger;
- insulated transport;
- backup power/alarm.

A freezer without monitoring is not a complete cold chain.

## 61.66 Backup Power

Backup can protect:

- frozen pollen;
- royal jelly;
- refrigerated products;
- digital inventory.

Compare generator, battery, alarm, or emergency-transfer options according to scale.

## 61.67 Water and Cleaning Equipment

Budget for:

- sinks;
- hoses;
- hot-water system;
- brushes;
- squeegees;
- food-safe detergents;
- drying racks;
- wastewater handling.

## 61.68 Pest Control Equipment

Facilities may need:

- insect screens;
- sealed doors;
- rodent-proof storage;
- monitoring traps;
- shelving.

Prevention costs less than contaminated product.

## 61.69 Signage and Labelling Equipment

Operational labels include:

- batch ID;
- HOLD/RELEASED status;
- chemical labels;
- equipment ID;
- allergen/product segregation.

A label printer may be a control tool, not merely marketing equipment.

## 61.70 Cost per Colony

Divide apiary-specific equipment cost by productive colony count carefully.

Do not dilute costs using colonies that are not actually productive without explaining the calculation.

## 61.71 Cost per Kilogram

For processing equipment:

`annual equipment cost ÷ annual kg processed`

can help compare options.

Annual equipment cost can include depreciation, maintenance, energy, and finance.

## 61.72 Cost per Jar

Packing cost per jar may include:

- jar;
- lid;
- label;
- tamper seal;
- carton;
- packing labour;
- filler depreciation;
- payment/fulfilment costs.

## 61.73 Make-or-Buy Analysis

Compare in-house processing with outsourcing using:

- variable cost;
- fixed cost;
- minimum volume;
- quality control;
- capacity;
- cash risk.

## 61.74 Break-Even Volume for a Machine

If owning a filler costs €A annually and outsourcing saves €B per jar, the rough break-even volume is:

`A ÷ B`

Use current local prices.

## 61.75 Currency Risk

Imported equipment prices can change with exchange rates.

Quotes should specify:

- currency;
- validity period;
- shipping;
- duties/taxes;
- delivery terms.

## 61.76 Shipping Cost

Bulky hive equipment can have high freight cost relative to item price.

Always compare landed cost, not catalogue price.

## 61.77 Import Duties and Compliance

Imported electrical/food-contact equipment may require:

- customs;
- product conformity;
- electrical safety;
- food-contact documentation.

Cheap non-compliant equipment can be unusable commercially.

## 61.78 VAT/Sales Tax

Whether a business can reclaim input tax depends on local tax registration and rules.

Budget using the amount the business will actually pay after applicable recovery—not assumptions.

## 61.79 Quotation Sheet

For major purchases compare at least:

- purchase price;
- freight;
- tax;
- installation;
- warranty;
- spare parts;
- energy;
- throughput;
- cleaning time;
- resale value.

## 61.80 Capacity Headroom

Buying exactly today's required capacity may force replacement after small growth.

Reasonable headroom can be economical.

Excessive headroom is idle capital.

## 61.81 Modular Growth

Modular systems can grow by adding:

- tanks;
- pump;
- filling head;
- freezer capacity;
- supers.

This can reduce large premature investments.

## 61.82 Purchase Timing

Buy equipment before it becomes an emergency but after the business case is proven.

Lead times can be months for specialised machinery.

## 61.83 Seasonal Discounts

Off-season buying may reduce cost, but avoid buying because of a discount alone.

Unused equipment at 30% discount is still wasted capital.

## 61.84 Warranties

Review:

- duration;
- commercial-use exclusions;
- shipping of parts;
- service location;
- electrical modifications that void warranty.

## 61.85 Resale Value

Standard equipment from established manufacturers may retain more value.

Custom equipment can have low resale value even if expensive to build.

## 61.86 Decision Table

| Situation | Cost interpretation | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Cheap extractor, slow and hard to clean | High labour/hygiene TCO | Compare full ownership cost |
| Large extractor used 5 hours/year | Low utilisation | Rent/share/outsource may be better |
| Repeated lifting injuries | Hidden labour/safety cost | Evaluate mechanical aids |
| Old brood equipment offered cheaply | Disease/residue risk | Reject or only use under validated control |
| Cold product expands faster than freezer space | Capacity bottleneck | Add cold capacity before more production |
| Packaging stock fills warehouse | Cash tied in inventory | Reduce order size/standardise packs |
| One pump seal can stop harvest | Downtime risk | Stock critical spare |

## 61.87 Practical Costing Worksheet

For every major item record:

- item;
- required capacity;
- purchase price;
- freight/tax;
- installation;
- useful life;
- annual maintenance;
- energy/consumables;
- annual utilisation;
- labour saving;
- expected resale value;
- financing cost;
- critical-spare cost.

Then calculate annual ownership cost and cost per unit processed.

## Key Takeaways

- Equipment should be costed as a system, not as isolated catalogue items.
- Current local quotations are more useful than static prices in a book.
- Spare capacity and spare parts are part of commercial reliability.
- Purchase price is only one component of total cost of ownership.
- Owner labour, cleaning time, energy, maintenance, downtime, and finance can dominate equipment economics.
- Used hive equipment can carry disease and residue risks that exceed the purchase saving.
- Used food equipment requires known previous use and hygienic refurbishment.
- Extraction capacity must be balanced with uncapping, transfer, tanks, and packing.
- Mechanisation should solve a proven bottleneck or safety problem.
- A replacement reserve prevents equipment ageing from becoming a future cash crisis.

## Chapter Summary

Commercial beekeeping equipment should be purchased from a capacity plan rather than a shopping list. Begin with the business model, map the production flow, and identify the true bottleneck. A large extractor cannot solve slow uncapping, inadequate tanks, or weak sales.

The correct comparison is total cost of ownership. Purchase price, freight, tax, finance, energy, labour, cleaning, maintenance, downtime, calibration, and resale value all influence the economic result. Used or shared equipment can reduce capital, but disease, contamination, scheduling, and traceability risks must be included.

As the operation grows, safety and resilience become increasingly important. Lifting aids, fire controls, electrical protection, cold-chain monitoring, and critical spares may not directly increase honey yield, but they prevent injury and catastrophic downtime.

A disciplined equipment register, maintenance schedule, and replacement reserve turn machinery from emergency purchases into planned productive assets.
