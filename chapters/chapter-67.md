# Chapter 67 — Traceability

## Introduction

Traceability is the ability to reconstruct the history and movement of a product. In beekeeping, that means being able to answer practical questions quickly:

- Which apiary produced this jar?
- Which harvest and extraction batch did it come from?
- Which tank was used?
- Which veterinary treatments affected the source colonies?
- Which supplier provided the jars, lids, ingredients, or purchased honey?
- Which business customers received the affected lot?
- How many units remain in stock?
- If honey was blended, what proportion came from each source or country?

A traceability system does not need to be complicated, but it must be consistent. The goal is not to collect every possible fact. The goal is to preserve the links needed to investigate quality, food-safety, origin, disease, or commercial problems without relying on memory.

Under EU General Food Law, food businesses are required to identify the businesses that supplied them and the businesses they supplied: the familiar “one step back, one step forward” principle. Final consumers are generally not treated like business customers for that external-traceability requirement. Strong businesses go further by maintaining **internal traceability** linking incoming materials, production batches, tanks, pack lots, and dispatch records.

For honey, internal traceability became even more commercially important with the EU blend-origin rules applying from 14 June 2026. Where honey from multiple countries is blended, country shares must be supported by the operator's traceability documentation. In other words, origin percentages must be produced by records at blending time, not reconstructed later from memory.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- distinguish external and internal traceability;
- explain the one-step-back/one-step-forward principle;
- design practical apiary, colony, harvest, batch, tank, and pack lot codes;
- connect treatments and colony movements to food lots;
- define lot boundaries deliberately;
- preserve traceability through extraction, blending, storage, packing, wholesale, online sales, and farmers markets;
- perform mass-balance reconciliation;
- support country-origin percentage declarations for honey blends;
- design paper or digital records that remain usable during a recall;
- conduct and evaluate a mock recall;
- manage retained samples and chain of custody;
- understand how traceability reduces the scope and cost of incidents.

## 67.1 What Traceability Is

Traceability is the documented connection among:

- source;
- process;
- product;
- destination.

For a beekeeper this often begins with an apiary and ends with a customer-facing lot code.

A strong system allows both directions:

**backward:** jar → packing lot → bulk tank → extraction/harvest → apiary/source;

**forward:** apiary/source → harvest → tank → packed lot → business customers/channels.

## 67.2 External Traceability

External traceability identifies business relationships outside the operation.

Typical records include:

- supplier name and address;
- product supplied;
- supplier lot;
- quantity;
- delivery date;
- business customer name and address;
- product/lot supplied;
- quantity;
- dispatch date.

EU General Food Law is built around this one-step-back/one-step-forward approach.

## 67.3 Internal Traceability

Internal traceability connects steps within the business.

Examples:

- Apiary A → Harvest H1 → Extractor Batch E1 → Tank T3 → Pack Lot P7;
- Supplier Honey Lot S15 → Blend B4 → Tank T5 → Retail Lots P10 and P11;
- Pollen Trap Lot PT8 → Drying Batch D3 → Pack Lot POL6.

Internal traceability is what makes a narrow recall possible.

## 67.4 Why Legal Minimum Traceability May Not Be Enough

A legal system may only require direct suppliers and business customers, but a commercial beekeeper also needs to know:

- which colonies contributed;
- which treatment records applied;
- which extraction day was involved;
- which tank held the honey;
- whether a blend included purchased honey;
- which packaging batch was used;
- which retail channels still hold stock.

The stronger the internal chain, the smaller the uncertainty.

## 67.5 Traceability Does Not Require Tracking Every Final Consumer

For ordinary retail sales, recording the identity of every individual consumer is usually unnecessary and may create privacy problems.

Instead, retain:

- pack lot;
- sales channel;
- date range;
- quantity sold.

Online orders may naturally contain customer details because fulfilment requires them, but they should be handled under applicable data-protection rules.

## 67.6 Apiary Identification

Every apiary should have a stable identifier.

Examples:

- `AP01`;
- `NORTH-FARM`;
- `BRL-03`.

The code should remain stable even if colony numbers change.

Record:

- location;
- landowner/site agreement where relevant;
- installation/movement dates;
- local notes;
- regulatory registration number where required.

## 67.7 Colony Identification

A colony identifier supports health and production traceability.

Examples:

- `AP01-C01`;
- `AP01-C02`.

Do not reuse a deceased colony's identity for a completely different colony without a clear historical rule.

If equipment is reused, the colony record and equipment history should remain distinguishable.

## 67.8 Queen Identification

Where queen genetics or sales matter, record:

- queen ID;
- breeder/source;
- introduction date;
- year;
- mating status/source;
- replacement date;
- daughter-colony links where relevant.

This can support investigation of production, disease resistance, swarming, and breeding performance.

## 67.9 Treatment Traceability

A food lot should be traceable to treatment status.

If colonies AP01-C01 through C10 received a treatment on specified dates, later harvest records should show whether those colonies contributed to saleable honey and whether label restrictions or withdrawal requirements were satisfied.

This link protects food safety.

## 67.10 Colony Movement

Record movement of colonies between apiaries when relevant to:

- disease tracing;
- pollination contracts;
- varietal honey origin;
- pesticide exposure;
- regulatory movement requirements.

A harvest origin claim becomes weak if colony location during the relevant flow is unknown.

## 67.11 Harvest Lot

A harvest lot can be defined by a consistent rule, such as:

- one apiary on one harvest date;
- one floral crop from one apiary;
- one group of supers removed together.

Example:

`H-AP01-20260915`

The code itself matters less than consistency and uniqueness.

## 67.12 Extraction Batch

An extraction batch groups material processed together.

Record:

- harvest lots included;
- start/end time;
- extractor line;
- operator;
- moisture checks;
- destination tank;
- deviations.

If several harvest lots are mixed during extraction, the new batch becomes the traceability junction.

## 67.13 Tank Identification

Every bulk vessel should have a unique ID.

Examples:

- `T01`;
- `SS500-02`.

A tank record should show:

- product lot(s) added;
- mass added;
- date/time;
- removals;
- current balance;
- cleaning status.

Do not identify tanks only by physical position such as “left tank”.

## 67.14 Packing Lot

A packing lot links bulk product to consumer units.

Example:

`P-260916-A`

It may represent:

- one packing run;
- one tank and label version;
- one day or shift;
- another documented logical batch.

The lot boundary should be narrow enough to support practical recall but not so fragmented that records become unusable.

## 67.15 Lot Coding Principles

A good lot code is:

- unique;
- readable;
- durable;
- recorded in the batch sheet;
- not dependent on secret memory;
- short enough to print reliably;
- structured enough to search.

Avoid codes that become ambiguous after year changes.

## 67.16 Date Codes Are Useful but Not Sufficient Alone

A date can be part of a lot code, but two products packed on the same date may come from different tanks.

Add a batch suffix, tank code, product code, or sequential number as needed.

## 67.17 Lot Boundaries Are a Risk Decision

A larger lot simplifies paperwork but increases recall size.

A smaller lot narrows incidents but increases record complexity.

Choose boundaries based on:

- production scale;
- tank size;
- source diversity;
- product value;
- origin claims;
- recall capability.

## 67.18 Purchased Honey

For purchased bulk honey record:

- supplier;
- supplier lot;
- country/countries of origin;
- quantity;
- delivery date;
- certificate of analysis where used;
- seal/container condition;
- internal incoming lot;
- storage tank.

Never allow purchased honey to become anonymous once it enters your premises.

## 67.19 Packaging Traceability

High-risk packaging issues can justify recording:

- jar supplier lot;
- lid/closure lot;
- label version;
- tamper-seal batch.

This is especially useful if a supplier later reports a packaging defect.

## 67.20 Ingredient Traceability

For flavoured or composite products record every ingredient lot.

Example:

`Creamed honey lot CH20 + cinnamon lot CIN44 → product lot HC21`

This enables allergen and ingredient recalls.

## 67.21 Country-of-Origin Traceability

Origin claims must be supported by real source records.

For own honey:

- apiary country;
- harvest period;
- movement history.

For purchased honey:

- supplier country documentation;
- lot certificates/contracts;
- receipt quantity;
- blend use.

## 67.22 EU 2026 Blend Percentages

Under EU rules applying from 14 June 2026, countries in a honey blend are generally declared in descending order by weight together with their percentage contribution, with specified legal tolerances and limited options.

FSAI notes that the percentage basis is supported by the operator's traceability documentation.

This makes weighing and mass balance a label-control function.

## 67.23 Weigh at the Time of Blending

Do not calculate origin percentages after the tank is empty.

At blending, record:

- source lot;
- source country;
- mass added;
- total final mass;
- losses/rework as applicable.

Then calculate:

`country share (%) = country-origin mass ÷ final defined blend mass × 100`

Follow applicable tolerance and labelling rules.

## 67.24 Multiple Lots From the Same Country

If two source lots come from the same country, their masses can be aggregated for country-percentage calculations while retaining individual lot identity internally.

Do not discard the finer source detail.

## 67.25 Mass Balance

Mass balance compares inputs, outputs, stock, and expected process losses.

A simple batch equation is:

`Opening stock + inputs = packed output + closing stock + documented loss/rework`

Large unexplained differences can indicate:

- record error;
- spill;
- wrong scale;
- unrecorded addition;
- mislabelling;
- theft or diversion.

## 67.26 Why Mass Balance Matters for Authenticity

A business that claims to sell 2,000 kg of a specific origin after receiving only 800 kg of that origin has a traceability problem even before laboratory testing.

Mass balance is a powerful authenticity control.

## 67.27 Yield Reconciliation

At harvest and extraction, compare:

- number of supers;
- gross weight;
- extracted honey;
- cappings honey;
- residual honey;
- waste/spill.

Yield anomalies can reveal mistakes and equipment loss.

## 67.28 Chain of Custody

Chain of custody documents who controlled a sample or product through transfers.

It becomes especially important for:

- regulatory samples;
- authenticity disputes;
- pesticide incidents;
- legal complaints;
- insurance claims.

Record identity, time, person, seal, and transfer.

## 67.29 Laboratory Sample Identity

A laboratory result is only useful if it is tied to the right lot.

The submission should include:

- unique sample ID;
- lot;
- sample date;
- sampler;
- sampling point;
- requested tests;
- storage/transport conditions.

## 67.30 Retained Samples

Retained samples can support later comparison.

A retained-sample system defines:

- which batches are retained;
- sample size;
- container;
- storage;
- duration;
- disposal.

The sample should be labelled independently from the production container.

## 67.31 Paper Systems

Paper records can be reliable when they are:

- standardised;
- legible;
- dated;
- protected;
- filed logically;
- easy to retrieve.

Do not rely on loose notebook pages with undefined abbreviations.

## 67.32 Digital Systems

Digital traceability can improve:

- search;
- lot linking;
- inventory;
- origin calculations;
- recall speed;
- document backup.

It introduces risks such as:

- data loss;
- access problems;
- cyberattack;
- accidental overwriting;
- vendor lock-in.

Export and backup matter.

## 67.33 Hybrid Systems

A practical small business may use:

- paper apiary sheets;
- spreadsheet batch records;
- accounting software for customers;
- printed lot labels;
- cloud backups.

The system can be hybrid as long as the links are reliable.

## 67.34 Master Data

Define standard codes for:

- apiaries;
- products;
- tanks;
- suppliers;
- customers;
- ingredients;
- packaging;
- treatments.

Stable master data prevents spelling variations from breaking searches.

## 67.35 Record Corrections

Corrections should preserve history.

Paper:

- single-line strikeout;
- corrected entry;
- initials/date where required.

Digital:

- audit trail/version history where appropriate.

Do not erase inconvenient data silently.

## 67.36 Backdating

Records should be made when work occurs.

Backdating from memory creates uncertainty and can undermine legal evidence.

If a late entry is unavoidable, mark it honestly as a late correction with source evidence.

## 67.37 Business Customers

Maintain records for wholesale/business deliveries:

- customer;
- address/contact;
- product;
- lot;
- quantity;
- date;
- invoice/delivery reference.

This is central to one-step-forward traceability.

## 67.38 Final Consumers

At farmers markets or direct retail, individual final-consumer identities generally need not be recorded for basic one-step-forward traceability.

Instead record:

- event/channel;
- date;
- lots sold;
- quantities.

This tells you where affected product was exposed to consumers.

## 67.39 Online Orders

Online systems often contain detailed delivery data.

Link fulfilment to lot by:

- pick list;
- warehouse scan;
- packing log;
- order metadata.

Protect personal data and do not retain more than legitimately needed.

## 67.40 Farmers Market Traceability

Before the market, record:

- lot quantities loaded.

After the market, record:

- unsold quantity;
- damaged samples;
- returns;
- approximate units sold by lot.

This allows the business to estimate consumer exposure during a recall.

## 67.41 Wholesale Mixed-Lot Orders

If one wholesale shipment contains multiple lots, delivery documents should identify each lot and quantity.

Do not write only “100 jars honey” if traceability requires distinguishing 60 jars P1 and 40 jars P2.

## 67.42 Stock Location

Inventory should identify where product is stored:

- warehouse bay;
- pallet;
- shelf;
- tank;
- cold room;
- vehicle in transit.

A recall is slowed dramatically when stock location is unknown.

## 67.43 Quarantine Stock

Held or rejected stock should remain physically or digitally blocked.

Traceability must preserve:

- original lot;
- reason for hold;
- quantity;
- location;
- disposition.

Never create a new lot simply to erase a quality failure.

## 67.44 Rework

When permitted, rework must remain traceable.

Record:

- original lot;
- quantity reworked;
- receiving lot;
- reason;
- authorisation.

The receiving lot inherits relevant risk history.

## 67.45 Product Downgrade

If table honey becomes baker's/industrial product under applicable law, preserve original lot identity and create a documented status change.

A new commercial category does not erase source history.

## 67.46 Returns

Returned stock may have unknown storage or tampering history.

Record:

- customer/channel;
- original lot;
- return date;
- quantity;
- reason;
- seal condition;
- disposition.

Do not automatically return consumer-held food to saleable stock.

## 67.47 Complaint Traceability

Every complaint should link to:

- lot;
- purchase date/channel;
- product size;
- photos/sample where available;
- related complaints.

Multiple complaints from one lot can reveal a pattern.

## 67.48 Traceability and Food Incidents

In an incident, traceability answers two urgent questions:

1. Where did the problem come from?
2. Where did the affected product go?

Internal records then determine whether the incident affects:

- one jar;
- one pack lot;
- one tank;
- one harvest;
- multiple countries/suppliers.

## 67.49 Withdrawal Scope

A weak system forces a wider withdrawal because uncertainty cannot be separated from affected product.

Better traceability can reduce:

- quantity destroyed;
- customer disruption;
- reputational damage;
- investigation time.

Traceability is therefore an economic asset.

## 67.50 Recall Team

Even a small business should define who will:

- stop dispatch;
- identify stock;
- contact authority;
- contact business customers;
- update website/social channels if required;
- reconcile quantity;
- preserve evidence.

One person may hold several roles, but responsibilities should be clear.

## 67.51 Recall Contact List

Keep current contacts for:

- competent food authority;
- laboratory;
- insurer;
- major customers;
- logistics provider;
- packaging supplier;
- legal/professional adviser where used.

Do not build the contact list during the emergency.

## 67.52 Mock Recall Design

Choose a realistic scenario, for example:

- pesticide residue in purchased honey;
- glass found in a packing lot;
- incorrect allergen label;
- country-origin documentation error.

Start with one lot code and trace both directions.

## 67.53 Mock Recall Metrics

Measure:

- time to identify source;
- time to identify business customers;
- percentage of quantity reconciled;
- time to obtain contact records;
- ability to stop online/warehouse dispatch;
- communication accuracy.

Set improvement actions.

## 67.54 Quantity Reconciliation

For affected lot L:

`produced quantity = stock on hand + business-customer quantity + direct-consumer quantity estimate + samples/waste + documented loss`

Aim to explain the full quantity.

Small unexplained variance can be normal depending on system; large variance requires investigation.

## 67.55 Recall Versus Withdrawal Records

Record why the chosen action was taken and who authorised it.

Include:

- hazard assessment;
- consumer exposure;
- regulatory advice;
- affected lot;
- communications;
- recovery/destruction quantities.

## 67.56 Destruction Records

If product is destroyed, record:

- lot;
- quantity;
- method;
- date;
- witness/contractor where relevant;
- waste documentation.

Destroyed product should not remain counted as available inventory.

## 67.57 Supplier Recall

If a supplier recalls jars, ingredients, or purchased honey:

- identify incoming supplier lot;
- identify all internal batches that used it;
- stop affected stock;
- identify customers;
- follow supplier/regulator instructions.

This tests internal traceability especially strongly.

## 67.58 Packaging Recall

A lid or jar defect can affect product across several honey lots.

Packaging-lot records allow you to identify all food lots using that component.

## 67.59 Treatment-Record Incident

If an authorised treatment was accidentally used while supers were present:

trace:

- affected colonies;
- supers/harvests;
- extraction batches;
- tanks;
- packed lots;
- customers.

Do not assume the whole year's production is affected if records can narrow the scope.

## 67.60 Pesticide Incident Traceability

Record:

- affected apiary;
- exposure date;
- flowering crop/area;
- colonies;
- supers harvested before/after event;
- samples;
- later lot disposition.

This separates exposed from unrelated product.

## 67.61 Disease Traceability

Animal-health traceability can intersect food traceability.

For AFB or other serious disease, records of:

- colony movements;
- brood-frame transfers;
- equipment transfers;
- honey feeding;
- sales of colonies

can help official tracing.

## 67.62 Equipment Traceability

For critical equipment, record:

- equipment ID;
- maintenance;
- repairs;
- lubricant;
- breakdowns;
- calibration.

If a metal fragment is found, equipment records help identify the likely machine and affected time window.

## 67.63 Cleaning Traceability

For high-risk transitions, record:

- equipment;
- lot ended;
- cleaning performed;
- verification;
- next lot.

This matters especially when allergens or flavoured products are processed.

## 67.64 Label Version Control

Each label design should have a version or approval reference.

Packing records should indicate which version was used.

This prevents a legal change or allergen correction from leaving old labels in uncontrolled use.

## 67.65 Best-Before and Lot Codes

A best-before date and lot code may be related, but they serve different functions.

The lot identifies production history; the date communicates shelf-life information under applicable law.

Do not rely on best-before alone if multiple batches share the same date.

## 67.66 Barcode and QR Systems

Barcodes and QR codes can improve scanning and consumer information.

They do not create traceability by themselves.

The database behind the code must remain accurate and available.

## 67.67 GS1-Type Systems

Larger businesses may use standardised trade-item and logistic-unit identifiers.

Benefits include interoperability with retailers and distribution centres.

Small businesses can still use simpler internal codes until commercial requirements justify standardisation.

## 67.68 Privacy

Traceability and privacy should be designed together.

Keep food-business records necessary for safety and legal obligations, but avoid using customer personal data beyond legitimate purposes.

Online customer data requires appropriate security and retention controls.

## 67.69 Cybersecurity

Protect digital traceability with:

- unique accounts;
- strong authentication;
- role-based access where practical;
- backups;
- tested recovery;
- protected exports;
- secure devices.

A ransomware event during a recall can become a food-safety problem.

## 67.70 Backup Strategy

Use at least one recoverable backup independent of the primary working system.

Periodically test:

- restore;
- export;
- readability;
- completeness.

A backup that has never been restored is only an assumption.

## 67.71 Data Retention

Retention periods depend on:

- food law;
- tax law;
- shelf life;
- customer contracts;
- insurance;
- certification.

Set a documented schedule rather than deleting records randomly.

## 67.72 Record Availability

Records should be retrievable promptly during inspection or incident.

A theoretically complete archive that requires several days to decipher is weak operational traceability.

## 67.73 Internal Audit

Audit one randomly chosen finished lot:

- trace backward to apiary/supplier;
- identify treatment and quality records;
- identify packaging/label version;
- reconcile quantity;
- trace forward to customers/channels.

Then audit a raw material forward.

## 67.74 Traceability Decision Table

| Situation | Traceability question | Required record |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer reports glass | Which packing lots/equipment window? | Pack lot, line/equipment, time, customers |
| Supplier recalls lids | Which food lots used that lid batch? | Packaging receipt + pack batch records |
| AFB found in apiary | Which colonies/equipment moved? | Colony and movement records |
| Country-origin challenge | What source masses formed blend? | Supplier lots + blend weights |
| High HMF in one tank | What source/process history? | Harvest/extraction/warming/tank records |
| Online recall | Which orders received lot? | Order-to-lot fulfilment link |
| Market recall | Which event sold affected lot? | Load/return/sales-by-lot record |
| Lost label on drum | Can identity be proven from controlled records? | Container/location/transfer records |

## 67.75 Example Lot Coding System

A small operation might use:

- apiary: `A03`;
- harvest: `H-A03-260815`;
- extraction: `E-260816-01`;
- tank: `T02`;
- packing: `P-260901-A`.

The packing sheet then links `P-260901-A` to `T02`, which links to the source extraction and harvest.

The exact syntax is optional; the links are essential.

## 67.76 Example Blend Record

| Source lot | Country | Mass added |
|---|---|---:|
| S1 | Ireland | 400 kg |
| S2 | Romania | 300 kg |
| S3 | Spain | 300 kg |
| **Total** |  | **1,000 kg** |

Before losses/adjustments permitted by the applicable calculation basis, the nominal shares are 40%, 30%, and 30%.

Record actual legally relevant final calculations under current rules.

## 67.77 Example Market Reconciliation

Before market:

- P1: 40 jars;
- P2: 30 jars.

After market:

- P1 returned: 12;
- P2 returned: 8;
- samples/damage: 2 jars equivalent documented.

The remainder can be assigned to direct-consumer sales with reasonable lot-level precision.

## 67.78 Example Online Fulfilment Link

Order `O-12345`:

- 2 × 340 g honey;
- lot P7;
- packed by operator X;
- dispatch date;
- carrier reference.

If lot P7 is recalled, the customer can be contacted where lawful and necessary.

## Scientific and Management Notes

### Traceability reduces uncertainty rather than eliminating hazards

A perfect lot code does not prevent contamination. It reduces the time and scope required to identify and control affected product.

### Granularity has economic value

Finer internal traceability can reduce recall cost by separating affected from unaffected product. However, extremely fine lot fragmentation can create administrative error. The optimal system balances precision and usability.

### Mass balance is an authenticity tool

Origin or premium-product claims are constrained by physical quantity. A business cannot legitimately sell more material of a defined origin than the traceable material received or produced, adjusted for documented processing flows.

### Data integrity matters as much as data volume

Ten accurate, linked records are more useful than hundreds of inconsistent fields. Traceability design should prioritise reliable links.

### Traceability should be tested under time pressure

Mock recalls reveal problems that ordinary record review misses: missing phone numbers, inaccessible cloud accounts, unknown warehouse locations, and unrecorded rework.

## Key Takeaways

- Traceability connects source, process, product, and destination.
- One-step-back/one-step-forward external traceability is the legal foundation in EU General Food Law.
- Internal traceability determines how narrowly a business can isolate an incident.
- Apiary, harvest, extraction, tank, and packing codes should be stable and linked.
- Treatment status and colony movements can be relevant to food and animal-health tracing.
- Blend-country percentage labels require real weight and origin documentation at blending time.
- Mass balance supports authenticity, inventory, and recall.
- Final consumers normally do not need individual recording for basic external traceability, but sales channel and lot exposure should be known.
- Digital systems require backup, access control, and recovery testing.
- A mock recall is the best practical test of whether traceability works.

## Chapter Summary

Traceability is the memory of a beekeeping business. A finished jar should be able to lead back through its pack lot, bulk tank, processing batch, harvest, apiary, treatment status, and purchased inputs. In the other direction, a problematic supplier lot or apiary event should lead forward to every affected tank, pack lot, wholesale customer, online order, and direct-sale channel.

EU food law establishes one-step-back/one-step-forward traceability, while internal traceability is designed by the operator. Strong internal links reduce recall scope and make origin claims defensible. The 2026 EU honey-origin rules make this especially important for blends because country percentage shares depend on traceability documentation.

Mass balance strengthens the system by ensuring that physical quantities reconcile. Retained samples, chain-of-custody records, label version control, packaging traceability, and customer delivery records further improve investigation quality.

The ultimate test is a mock recall. If a business can identify source, affected stock, business customers, direct-sale channels, and reconciled quantities within a reasonable time without relying on one person's memory, the traceability system is becoming operationally reliable.
