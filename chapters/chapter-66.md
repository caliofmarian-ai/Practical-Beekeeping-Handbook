# Chapter 66 — Food Safety

## Introduction

Food safety begins before honey enters a jar. It begins with the materials placed in the hive, the medicines used on colonies, the water used to clean equipment, the fuel burned in the smoker, the cleanliness of harvest boxes, the condition of the extraction room, the design of storage containers, and the accuracy of the records that connect a finished product back to its source.

Honey is naturally hostile to the growth of many microorganisms because of its high sugar concentration, low water activity, acidity, and other antimicrobial properties. This natural stability is valuable, but it must not be confused with sterility or with exemption from food law. Honey can still be contaminated by chemicals, physical foreign bodies, excessive moisture, dirty equipment, unsafe ingredients, packaging failures, or naturally occurring spores. Other hive products such as fresh pollen and royal jelly have very different moisture and preservation requirements and can be substantially more perishable.

A professional food-safety system therefore asks four questions continuously:

1. What can harm the consumer?
2. Where can that hazard enter, survive, increase, or remain uncontrolled?
3. What preventive control stops or reduces it?
4. What evidence proves the control is working?

This chapter uses HACCP-style reasoning without pretending that every small beekeeper needs an industrial food factory. The scale of controls should match the operation, but the responsibility for safe food remains.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- distinguish food safety from quality, authenticity, and marketing;
- identify biological, chemical, physical, and allergen hazards in beekeeping products;
- explain prerequisite programmes and HACCP-based control;
- map hazards from hive to harvest, extraction, processing, storage, packing, transport, online fulfilment, and market sales;
- design hygiene zones and food-contact controls;
- prevent contamination from veterinary medicines, pesticides, fuel, smoke, cleaning chemicals, water, glass, metal, pests, and dirty equipment;
- understand product-specific risks in honey, pollen, royal jelly, propolis, creamed products, comb honey, and flavoured products;
- distinguish cleaning from sanitation and verify both;
- create corrective-action, hold, withdrawal, and recall procedures;
- understand current Irish/EU food-business registration and HACCP expectations as jurisdiction-specific examples.

## 66.1 Food Safety Is Not the Same as Quality

A product can be safe but low quality. It can also look premium while being unsafe.

Examples:

- coarse crystallisation is mainly a quality issue;
- fermented honey can be a quality and legal-compliance issue;
- glass in a jar is a physical food-safety hazard;
- unauthorised veterinary-medicine residue is a chemical hazard;
- incorrect country-of-origin wording is primarily a legal/traceability issue;
- a false therapeutic claim is a legal/marketing issue;
- undeclared allergenic ingredients in flavoured honey can become a food-safety issue.

Separating these categories prevents weak controls and confused corrective action.

## 66.2 The Four Main Hazard Categories

### Biological hazards

Relevant examples include:

- osmotolerant yeasts in high-moisture honey;
- mould or yeast growth in poorly dried pollen;
- microbial growth in water-based propolis products;
- microbial deterioration of inadequately chilled royal jelly;
- naturally occurring bacterial spores;
- pests contaminating exposed food.

### Chemical hazards

Examples include:

- veterinary-medicine residues;
- pesticide residues;
- cleaning chemicals;
- fuel and lubricants;
- smoke contaminants;
- heavy metals;
- unauthorised additives;
- migration from unsuitable food-contact materials.

### Physical hazards

Examples include:

- glass;
- metal fragments;
- broken plastic;
- wood splinters;
- stones;
- insects;
- brush bristles;
- packaging fragments.

### Allergen hazards

Plain honey is not normally formulated with the regulated allergenic ingredients used in composite foods, but allergen hazards become important when the same facility handles:

- nuts;
- milk powders;
- sesame;
- cereal ingredients;
- flavoured spreads;
- other composite foods.

Bee-product sensitivity also exists, particularly for pollen and propolis, although legal allergen-labelling categories vary by jurisdiction.

## 66.3 Natural Stability Does Not Mean Sterility

Honey can contain microorganisms and spores while still resisting active growth.

A key public-health example is *Clostridium botulinum* spores. Public-health authorities advise against feeding honey to infants under 12 months because infant intestinal conditions can allow spores to germinate.

Routine honey extraction, straining, warming, or creaming does not reliably sterilise honey of these spores.

This is a useful reminder: low water activity suppresses growth; it does not prove absence.

## 66.4 Product Risk Varies Across the Hive-Product Range

| Product | Typical stability challenge | Important control |
|---|---|---|
| Mature honey | Moisture, chemical/physical contamination | Dry hygienic handling, moisture control, traceability |
| Creamed honey | Moisture redistribution, fermentation, ingredients | Base-honey quality, stable crystallisation, formulation control |
| Comb honey | Heat damage, pests, contamination of edible wax | Fresh broodless comb, food-safe storage, pest control |
| Pollen | High moisture, microbes, pesticides, allergens | Rapid chilling/freezing or validated drying, clean traps |
| Royal jelly | Highly perishable, temperature-sensitive | Cold chain, hygiene, short controlled exposure |
| Propolis | Chemical variability, contaminants, solvent/formulation hazards | Clean collection, defined extraction, contaminant control |
| Beeswax for food/cosmetic chain | Residues/adulteration | Source segregation, end-use specification, testing |

The food-safety plan must therefore be product-specific.

## 66.5 The Food-Safety Management System

A food-safety management system should be a working system rather than a folder kept for inspections.

Its components commonly include:

- prerequisite programmes;
- hazard analysis;
- control measures;
- monitoring;
- corrective action;
- verification;
- records;
- traceability;
- withdrawal/recall procedure;
- staff training.

In Ireland, FSAI describes HACCP-based procedures as a legal requirement for food businesses and requires registration before a food business begins operating. The exact competent authority and obligations depend on the activity. These are current Irish examples, not universal rules for every country.

## 66.6 Prerequisite Programmes

Prerequisite programmes create the hygienic foundation on which HACCP depends.

Common prerequisites include:

- approved suppliers;
- suitable premises;
- potable water;
- equipment maintenance;
- cleaning and sanitation;
- pest control;
- personal hygiene;
- staff training;
- waste management;
- chemical control;
- glass/brittle-plastic control;
- temperature control where required;
- allergen control;
- traceability;
- calibration of measuring equipment.

If these foundations are weak, a complicated HACCP chart will not rescue the operation.

## 66.7 HACCP Logic

HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point.

The practical logic is:

1. describe the product and process;
2. map the process steps;
3. identify reasonably foreseeable hazards;
4. evaluate their likelihood and severity;
5. identify effective controls;
6. determine whether any step is a critical control point or whether the hazard is controlled through prerequisites/operational controls;
7. define limits or acceptance criteria;
8. monitor;
9. take corrective action when control fails;
10. verify the system;
11. keep records.

Small operations can use simple forms, but the reasoning must still be real.

## 66.8 Not Every Control Is a Critical Control Point

Over-labelling every step as a CCP makes the system unusable.

Examples of controls that may be managed as prerequisite programmes include:

- handwashing;
- pest-proof doors;
- cleaning schedules;
- supplier approval;
- glass control.

A true CCP requires a specific point where loss of control can create unacceptable food-safety risk and where control can be monitored effectively.

The exact classification depends on the process.

## 66.9 Process Mapping: Hive to Consumer

A useful flow diagram may include:

**apiary inputs → harvest → covered transport → receiving → extraction → straining/settling → processing → bulk storage → packing → finished storage → dispatch → retail/online/market sale**

For other products add product-specific steps such as:

- pollen trapping → immediate chilling/freezing/drying;
- royal-jelly grafting/collection → immediate cold chain;
- propolis trap harvest → cleaning → extraction/formulation.

Walk the actual process rather than copying a generic flow chart.

## 66.10 Hive Inputs Are Food-Safety Inputs

Anything deliberately placed into a food-producing hive can affect later products.

Control:

- veterinary medicines;
- feed;
- foundation/wax;
- hive paint/coatings;
- cleaning materials;
- pest-control products;
- smoker fuels.

The cheapest hive input can become the most expensive contamination event.

## 66.11 Veterinary Medicines

Before using any veterinary or mite-control product, verify:

- legal authorisation;
- target condition;
- label dose;
- placement;
- temperature restrictions;
- honey-super restrictions;
- withdrawal periods;
- required records;
- disposal instructions.

Do not use agricultural formulations simply because they contain a similar active ingredient.

## 66.12 Treatment Records

Treatment records should identify:

- apiary and colony/group;
- product;
- active ingredient;
- batch/lot where relevant;
- application and removal dates;
- person applying;
- supers present/absent;
- withdrawal/release date;
- deviations.

A harvest should not proceed if treatment status cannot be established.

## 66.13 Feed and Honey Contamination

Feeding sugar syrup while marketable honey supers are on can create product-identity and quality problems if feed reaches harvest comb.

Feeding unknown honey can also move bee diseases such as AFB spores.

Use feed deliberately, document timing, and protect saleable crop identity.

## 66.14 Smoker Fuel

Use clean suitable smoker fuel.

Avoid:

- painted timber;
- treated wood;
- plastic;
- oil-soaked material;
- synthetic fabrics;
- chemical packaging.

Excess smoke can taint honey and comb even when no acute toxic hazard is created.

## 66.15 Agricultural Pesticide Exposure

Suspected pesticide contamination requires more than a bee-mortality response.

If honey or pollen may be affected:

- identify and segregate relevant lots;
- record application/exposure history if known;
- preserve samples;
- contact appropriate authority/laboratory when required;
- do not blend suspect product into unaffected stock.

## 66.16 Harvest Hygiene

At harvest:

- cover supers immediately;
- keep them off soil and dirty surfaces;
- prevent rain entry;
- control robbing;
- exclude brood comb from ordinary honey extraction;
- use clean food-contact brushes/covers;
- use a clean vehicle.

The apiary is the first food-handling site.

## 66.17 Transport Hygiene

Transport vehicles should be:

- clean;
- dry;
- free from fuel leakage;
- free from chemical cargo contamination;
- protected from rain and dust;
- secured against load shift.

Never transport open food supers beside pesticides, waste, fuel cans, or dirty livestock equipment.

## 66.18 Receiving Controls

At the extraction room, check:

- lot identity;
- covers intact;
- no brood frames;
- no rainwater;
- no chemical odour;
- no gross contamination;
- no pest infestation;
- no leaking/broken comb that could contaminate surrounding material.

Questionable supers should be held separately.

## 66.19 Hygiene Zoning

A simple honey room benefits from logical separation between:

- incoming field-exposed equipment;
- clean food-contact processing;
- packaging materials;
- chemical storage;
- waste.

One-way product movement reduces cross-contamination.

## 66.20 Food-Contact Surfaces

Food-contact surfaces should be:

- suitable for food;
- smooth;
- durable;
- cleanable;
- corrosion-resistant;
- maintained in good repair.

Common suitable materials include appropriate stainless steel and certified food-grade plastics.

Unknown metals, damaged coatings, or reused chemical containers are poor choices.

## 66.21 Water

Water used where it can contact food or food-contact surfaces should be potable or otherwise meet the legal suitability standard for the operation.

Water can introduce:

- microbes;
- chemicals;
- excess moisture.

Honey equipment must be fully dry before use after wet cleaning.

## 66.22 Honey Is Hygroscopic

Honey absorbs moisture from humid air.

Food-safety implications include:

- increased fermentation risk;
- local wet zones under condensation;
- dilution from wet equipment;
- moisture gain during long open-tank exposure.

A dry honey room is as important as a clean one.

## 66.23 Moisture and Fermentation

As honey moisture increases, osmotolerant yeasts can grow more readily.

Control includes:

- harvesting mature honey;
- representative refractometer measurement;
- keeping rooms dry;
- protecting open tanks;
- avoiding water ingress;
- segregating wet frames/lots;
- suitable storage temperature.

Legal moisture limits should not be confused with the optimum safety margin for every lot.

## 66.24 Cleaning Versus Sanitation

**Cleaning** removes food residues, wax, propolis, dirt, and organic matter.

**Sanitation/disinfection** reduces microorganisms to an acceptable level where required.

A dirty surface cannot be made hygienic simply by spraying sanitiser over honey and wax residues.

Remove soil first.

## 66.25 Cleaning Sequence

A controlled sequence can include:

1. remove bulk honey/wax dry;
2. dismantle equipment as needed;
3. wash with suitable detergent;
4. rinse where required;
5. sanitise where required by the process;
6. drain and dry fully;
7. inspect before use.

The exact method must suit the equipment and product.

## 66.26 Cleaning Chemicals

Cleaning chemicals should be:

- labelled;
- stored away from food;
- used at correct concentration;
- compatible with equipment;
- rinsed when required;
- handled with PPE;
- never decanted into food containers without unmistakable control.

Chemical residues can destroy an entire batch.

## 66.27 Cleaning Verification

Verification can include:

- visual inspection;
- no sticky residue;
- no chemical odour;
- complete dryness;
- ATP or microbiological testing where appropriate;
- documented pre-operational checks.

A signed checklist should represent an actual inspection.

## 66.28 Pest Control

Food rooms should exclude and monitor:

- rodents;
- flies;
- ants;
- cockroaches;
- wasps/bees;
- stored-product pests.

Use integrated pest management that prevents pesticide contamination of exposed products.

## 66.29 Doors and Openings

Control entry through:

- self-closing or managed doors;
- screens;
- sealed gaps;
- clean waste practices;
- prompt removal of spills.

Open honey is a powerful insect attractant.

## 66.30 Glass Control

Broken glass in viscous honey is difficult to remove confidently.

A glass-control programme can:

- minimise unnecessary glass in processing areas;
- identify essential glass items;
- inspect lights or use protected fittings;
- define a breakage response;
- quarantine exposed product.

Do not strain and release honey after a glass break simply because visible pieces were removed.

## 66.31 Brittle Plastic

Hard plastic can fragment like glass.

Inspect:

- lids;
- guards;
- scrapers;
- measuring tools;
- buckets;
- light covers.

Replace cracked items before they shed fragments.

## 66.32 Metal Control

Metal fragments can come from:

- damaged strainers;
- extractor baskets;
- knives;
- screws;
- wire;
- machinery wear.

Preventive maintenance and visual inspection are more valuable than trying to find metal after it enters honey.

## 66.33 Lubricants

Use food-grade lubricants where incidental food contact is possible.

Control:

- quantity;
- application point;
- drip shields;
- maintenance records;
- damaged seals.

Automotive grease has no place in a food-contact zone.

## 66.34 Personnel Hygiene

Food handlers should understand:

- handwashing;
- wound covering;
- clean clothing;
- illness reporting;
- no eating/smoking over food;
- safe jewellery policy;
- hair control where required;
- cross-contamination prevention.

Small family businesses need the same discipline even when nobody is called an “employee”.

## 66.35 Handwashing

Handwashing requires:

- suitable water;
- soap;
- hygienic drying;
- accessible sink/location;
- correct timing.

Gloves do not replace handwashing and can spread contamination when worn carelessly.

## 66.36 Illness

Food handlers with vomiting, diarrhoea, infectious skin lesions, or other relevant illness should follow current food-handler exclusion/reporting rules.

Honey may be low risk for microbial growth, but dirty hands can still contaminate packaging, ingredients, and high-moisture products.

## 66.37 Training

Training should match the task.

Examples:

- apiary workers: treatment/harvest separation;
- honey-room workers: hygiene and foreign-body control;
- market staff: sampling and allergen communication;
- online fulfilment staff: lot selection and damaged-package control;
- managers: HACCP, recall, and traceability.

Training is effective only when behaviour is observed and corrected.

## 66.38 Packing Controls

Before packing:

- product is released;
- lot identity is known;
- jars/containers are clean and suitable;
- closures are correct;
- labels match the product;
- net quantity controls are working;
- foreign-body and allergen risks are controlled.

A label error can turn safe food into a legally non-compliant product.

## 66.39 Packaging Materials

Packaging should be:

- food-contact compliant;
- appropriate for honey acidity and shelf life;
- clean;
- protected in storage;
- traceable where necessary.

Reuse is not automatically safe simply because a container “looks clean”.

## 66.40 Tamper Evidence

Tamper-evident features can improve product integrity and customer confidence.

They do not replace hygiene or secure packaging.

A damaged seal should trigger investigation of the individual unit or lot as appropriate.

## 66.41 Honey Sampling at Markets

Sampling creates new hazards because sealed food becomes exposed repeatedly.

Control:

- clean serving utensils;
- covered sample food;
- single-use sticks/spoons where appropriate;
- no double-dipping;
- hand hygiene;
- allergen information for added ingredients;
- waste disposal.

Do not leave an open communal honey jar with unrestricted consumer contact.

## 66.42 Flavoured Honey Products

Adding ingredients changes the hazard profile.

Potential new hazards include:

- allergens;
- extra moisture;
- microbial spores;
- undeclared ingredients;
- cross-contact;
- product-name changes.

A plain-honey HACCP plan is not sufficient for a honey-and-nut spread.

## 66.43 Fresh Fruit in Honey

Fresh fruit can add enough water to support microbial growth.

Do not assume honey automatically preserves high-moisture additions safely.

A formulated product requires validated water-activity, pH, process, preservation, and shelf-life controls as appropriate.

## 66.44 Dried Ingredients

Dry ingredients can introduce:

- allergens;
- spores;
- foreign bodies;
- pesticide residues;
- moisture.

Use approved suppliers and specifications.

## 66.45 Pollen Food Safety

Fresh pollen is much more perishable than honey.

Control priorities include:

- clean traps;
- frequent harvest;
- rapid cooling/freezing or validated drying;
- water-activity/moisture control;
- pest exclusion;
- pesticide/residue awareness;
- allergy warnings as required.

Do not leave fresh pollen warm in collection drawers for extended periods.

## 66.46 Royal Jelly Food Safety

Royal jelly is high-moisture and biologically active.

Control priorities include:

- hygienic collection tools;
- rapid cooling;
- validated chilled/frozen storage;
- minimal room-temperature exposure;
- clean packaging;
- cold-chain monitoring.

Temperature abuse can reduce quality even before obvious spoilage appears.

## 66.47 Propolis Products

Raw propolis is relatively dry, but extracts can have very different hazards depending on solvent.

Water-based propolis products can require microbiological preservation.

Alcohol-based products introduce:

- flammable-liquid safety;
- residual-solvent/strength specifications;
- packaging compatibility;
- labelling restrictions.

Food safety and workplace safety overlap.

## 66.48 Comb Honey

Comb honey requires protection of both honey and edible wax.

Important controls include:

- fresh broodless comb;
- no treatment contamination;
- moisture control;
- heat protection;
- food-safe wax-moth control;
- clean packaging;
- no pesticide exposure during storage.

## 66.49 Beeswax for Food-Related Uses

Rendered apiary wax is not automatically food-additive or cosmetic grade.

Potential hazards include:

- acaricide residues;
- pesticide residues;
- adulterants;
- disease history;
- processing contaminants.

End-use specifications determine required testing.

## 66.50 Allergen Management

If regulated allergens enter the facility:

1. identify every allergenic ingredient;
2. verify supplier information;
3. store separately as needed;
4. schedule production to reduce cross-contact;
5. clean and verify;
6. label correctly;
7. manage rework carefully.

Do not rely on memory when multiple product recipes exist.

## 66.51 Allergen Labelling and Precautionary Statements

Mandatory allergen declaration and precautionary “may contain” practices depend on jurisdiction.

Precautionary wording should not be used casually as a substitute for proper cross-contact control.

Consult current applicable food-information rules.

## 66.52 Infant Honey Risk

Honey should not be given to infants under 12 months because of infant botulism risk associated with spores.

This warning should be communicated accurately without implying that honey is unsafe for the general adult population.

Local label requirements differ.

## 66.53 Food Fraud as a Safety-System Concern

Adulteration can create food-safety as well as authenticity risks when unknown syrups, colours, medicines, or chemicals enter the food chain.

Supplier approval and traceability help prevent economically motivated adulteration.

A suspicious cheap bulk lot deserves more, not less, due diligence.

## 66.54 Approved Suppliers

For purchased inputs, maintain:

- supplier identity;
- product specification;
- food-contact status where relevant;
- lot numbers;
- certificates of analysis where appropriate;
- complaint history;
- audit/approval status for higher-risk supplies.

Supplier approval is a preventive control.

## 66.55 Incoming Inspection

Check incoming:

- jars/lids;
- labels;
- ingredients;
- bulk honey;
- seed honey;
- pollen/propolis;
- chemicals;
- food-contact equipment.

Inspect identity, lot, condition, documentation, and contamination risk.

## 66.56 Hold and Release

Use clear product status:

- HOLD;
- RELEASED;
- REJECTED;
- REWORK-ELIGIBLE.

A product awaiting a laboratory result should not be indistinguishable from saleable stock.

## 66.57 Corrective Action

A corrective action should answer:

1. What failed?
2. What product was affected?
3. Was food safety compromised?
4. What immediate containment was taken?
5. What is the disposition of affected product?
6. What caused the failure?
7. What prevents recurrence?
8. How will effectiveness be verified?

Do not stop at “operator retrained” when equipment design caused the failure.

## 66.58 Non-Conforming Product

Non-conforming product can result from:

- high moisture;
- wrong label;
- broken seal;
- residue concern;
- fermentation;
- foreign body;
- traceability failure;
- temperature abuse.

Segregate first; investigate second; release only with evidence.

## 66.59 Withdrawal Versus Recall

Terminology varies, but commonly:

- **withdrawal** removes product from the supply chain before it reaches consumers or when consumer action is not required;
- **recall** involves product that has reached consumers and may require public/customer action.

Follow the definitions and procedures of the competent authority in the relevant market.

## 66.60 Recall Readiness

A recall system should identify quickly:

- affected lot;
- quantity produced;
- stock on site;
- business customers supplied;
- consumer sales channels;
- source inputs;
- related lots;
- public communication needs.

Traceability determines the precision of the recall.

## 66.61 Mock Recalls

A mock recall tests the system without a real hazard.

Set a scenario such as:

> “Lot H260915A has a suspected chemical contamination.”

Then measure:

- time to identify source;
- time to identify customers;
- quantity reconciliation;
- completeness of records;
- communication readiness.

Fix weaknesses before a real incident.

## 66.62 Complaint Management

Consumer complaints can reveal hazards.

Prioritise complaints involving:

- glass/metal;
- chemical taste/odour;
- swelling/fermentation;
- allergic reaction;
- wrong label;
- suspected tampering.

Retain product and evidence where possible and link the complaint to the lot.

## 66.63 Food Incident Records

Record:

- date/time;
- product and lot;
- reporter;
- nature of hazard;
- quantity;
- distribution;
- immediate action;
- authority/customer contacts;
- sample/lab information;
- final disposition;
- corrective action.

Objective records are more useful than emotional descriptions.

## 66.64 Emergency: Glass Breakage

If glass breaks near exposed food:

1. stop activity;
2. isolate the area;
3. identify exposed product;
4. remove people safely;
5. clean using the defined glass procedure;
6. discard or hold any product whose freedom from glass cannot be assured;
7. inspect before restarting.

## 66.65 Emergency: Chemical Spill

Protect people first.

Then:

- stop food processing;
- isolate affected product/equipment;
- identify chemical;
- follow spill instructions;
- assess packaging/contact;
- clean and verify;
- discard food if safety cannot be demonstrated.

## 66.66 Emergency: Water Ingress

If water enters honey:

- stop transfer;
- isolate affected lot;
- identify water source and cleanliness;
- measure moisture;
- assess chemical/microbial contamination;
- do not blend the problem away.

## 66.67 Emergency: Power Failure

For honey, short power loss may have limited safety impact, but cold-chain products such as royal jelly can be affected rapidly.

Record:

- duration;
- product temperature;
- storage limits;
- corrective action.

Prioritise the most temperature-sensitive products.

## 66.68 Emergency: Refrigeration Failure

For royal jelly or other chilled products:

- keep doors closed;
- record time/temperature;
- move product to validated backup storage if safe;
- hold affected lots;
- assess against validated stability criteria.

Do not reset the thermometer and pretend the excursion did not happen.

## 66.69 Calibration

Critical measuring equipment may include:

- refractometers;
- thermometers;
- scales;
- pH meters;
- water-activity meters;
- data loggers.

Calibration/verification frequency should reflect risk and use.

## 66.70 Refractometer Control

A honey refractometer should be:

- clean;
- checked/calibrated according to manufacturer method;
- protected from water dilution;
- used with representative samples;
- recorded by instrument ID where the system requires it.

Instrument precision cannot fix poor sampling.

## 66.71 Thermometer Control

Verify thermometers against a suitable reference or calibration service according to risk.

The display setting of a warming cabinet is not always the same as the product temperature.

## 66.72 Scale Control

Scales used for legal net quantity may require specific verification under weights-and-measures law.

Process scales also need routine checks so formulation and mass-balance records remain reliable.

## 66.73 Documentation Control

Food-safety documents should have:

- title;
- version;
- date;
- owner;
- approved procedure;
- obsolete-copy control where necessary.

Staff should use the current procedure, not a three-year-old printed sheet from a drawer.

## 66.74 Record Integrity

Good records are:

- legible;
- contemporaneous;
- attributable;
- accurate;
- protected from unauthorised alteration;
- retrievable.

Backfilling records from memory weakens evidence.

## 66.75 Digital Systems

Digital records can improve:

- traceability;
- alerts;
- calibration reminders;
- document control;
- recall speed.

But they require:

- backups;
- access control;
- cybersecurity;
- data export/recovery plans.

A cloud outage should not erase the only copy of the food-safety system.

## 66.76 Internal Audits

An internal audit checks whether actual practice matches the system.

Audit examples:

- follow one honey lot backwards;
- inspect chemical storage;
- observe handwashing;
- test glass-break response;
- review expired treatment products;
- inspect pest-control records;
- verify cleaning results.

Audit evidence should drive corrective action.

## 66.77 Management Review

At least periodically, review:

- complaints;
- non-conformances;
- recalls/mock recalls;
- supplier issues;
- laboratory trends;
- pest activity;
- equipment failures;
- training needs;
- legal changes.

Food safety is a living management system.

## 66.78 Irish Example: Registration

Current FSAI guidance states that a food business must be registered before it starts operating, including a business operating from home. The registering authority depends on the type of activity.

The lesson for an international reader is broader: identify the competent authority before starting commercial food activity.

## 66.79 Irish Example: HACCP-Based Procedures

FSAI states that HACCP-based procedures are a legal requirement and should be supported by prerequisite programmes.

The system should suit the business; a microproducer may use a simpler documented system than an industrial packer, but simplicity does not mean no hazard analysis.

## 66.80 Irish/EU Example: Honey Rules

Honey composition, designation, and labelling are governed by specific honey legislation plus general food-information rules.

From 14 June 2026, EU-origin rules for blended honey became more specific, requiring countries and percentage shares under the applicable conditions.

Food safety, legal identity, and traceability therefore intersect.

## 66.81 Food-Safety Culture

Food-safety culture means people act safely even when nobody is watching.

It is strengthened by:

- clear expectations;
- practical training;
- managers following the same rules;
- easy reporting of problems;
- no punishment for stopping unsafe production;
- learning from near misses.

A culture of hiding mistakes creates larger incidents later.

## 66.82 Near Misses

A near miss is an event that could have caused contamination but was caught in time.

Examples:

- unlabelled chemical found beside jars before use;
- cracked plastic scoop replaced before fragments broke off;
- wrong label detected before dispatch;
- wet tank discovered before filling.

Record significant near misses because they reveal system weakness.

## 66.83 Food Defence

Food defence addresses deliberate contamination or tampering.

Small businesses should still consider:

- locked chemical storage;
- controlled access to bulk tanks;
- secure online admin accounts;
- tamper-evident closures;
- visitor control where appropriate.

## 66.84 Fraud Prevention

Food fraud prevention addresses economically motivated deception.

Controls include:

- approved suppliers;
- origin records;
- mass balance;
- authenticity testing based on risk;
- secure labels;
- segregation of non-compliant stock.

Food defence and food fraud overlap but are not identical.

## 66.85 Product Release

Before releasing a lot, verify applicable items such as:

- lot identity;
- moisture/specification;
- treatment release;
- label version;
- package integrity;
- required laboratory results;
- origin records;
- hold status cleared.

Release should be an intentional decision.

## 66.87 Practical Food-Safety Setup Procedure

1. List every product.
2. Draw the actual process flow for each product family.
3. List biological, chemical, physical, and allergen hazards at each step.
4. Identify prerequisites and product-specific controls.
5. Define monitoring and acceptance criteria.
6. Define hold/corrective-action procedures.
7. Build traceability and recall links.
8. Train everyone who performs the work.
9. Verify through observation, sampling, audits, and mock recall.
10. Review whenever product, equipment, supplier, law, or process changes.

## Scientific Notes

### Water activity is more directly linked to microbial growth than moisture percentage

Honey moisture is easy to measure and useful in routine control, but water activity better represents water available to microorganisms. Composition influences the relationship between the two.

### Osmotolerant yeasts exploit a niche most microbes cannot

Honey suppresses many microorganisms but selected yeasts tolerate high osmotic pressure. High moisture and warm storage therefore create a specific fermentation risk.

### Spores can remain without growth

Low-water foods can carry dormant bacterial spores. Safety advice for infant honey illustrates the difference between presence and growth.

### Hazard control should be risk-based

The same control intensity is not necessary for every product. Fresh pollen and royal jelly require more aggressive preservation controls than mature honey because their water activity and composition differ fundamentally.

### Verification tests the system, not only the product

A final laboratory result cannot replace hygienic design. Food-safety systems rely on preventing contamination rather than testing every jar after the event.

## Key Takeaways

- Honey's natural stability does not make it sterile or exempt from food-safety control.
- Food safety, quality, authenticity, and marketing legality are related but distinct.
- Biological, chemical, physical, and allergen hazards must be assessed across the real process.
- Prerequisite programmes create the foundation for HACCP-based control.
- Hive treatments, smoker fuel, harvest transport, water, chemicals, equipment, packaging, and ingredients can all affect food safety.
- Product risk varies greatly: royal jelly and fresh pollen are far more perishable than mature honey.
- Cleaning removes soil; sanitation is a separate microbial-control step where required.
- Hold and release status prevents questionable product from entering sale accidentally.
- Corrective action must address cause, not merely the immediate symptom.
- Traceability and mock recall determine how precisely a real food incident can be contained.

## Chapter Summary

Food safety in beekeeping is a chain of preventive decisions from hive to consumer. Honey's low water activity provides natural protection, but chemical residues, glass, fuel, cleaning agents, excess water, allergens, dirty equipment, packaging failures, and naturally occurring spores remain real hazards. Other hive products can be significantly more perishable and require dedicated preservation controls.

The strongest system begins with prerequisite programmes: suitable premises, food-contact equipment, water, cleaning, pest control, chemical control, staff hygiene, supplier approval, maintenance, and traceability. HACCP-based reasoning then identifies product-specific hazards, monitoring, corrective action, and verification.

Commercial food handling must also respond to legal requirements. In Ireland, current FSAI guidance requires food-business registration before operation and HACCP-based food-safety procedures. Other jurisdictions use different competent authorities and legal structures, so the method to teach is not memorisation of one country's form but maintenance of a current compliance map.

A food-safety system is successful when unsafe or uncertain product is stopped before release, incidents are traceable quickly, corrective action prevents recurrence, and everyday behaviour matches the written procedure.
