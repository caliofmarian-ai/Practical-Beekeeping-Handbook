# Chapter 56 — Propolis

## Introduction

Propolis is a resin-rich material made by honey bees from plant exudates, wax, and bee secretions. Colonies use it to seal cracks, narrow unwanted openings, smooth internal surfaces, stabilise hive structures, and modify the microbial environment of the nest.

For the beekeeper, propolis is both a hive material and a potential commercial product. Those roles create an important quality problem: material suitable for sealing a wooden hive is not automatically suitable for food, cosmetic, supplement, or research use.

Propolis composition varies dramatically with vegetation, season, geography, bee behaviour, and collection method. A temperate poplar-type propolis can have a very different chemical fingerprint from tropical green, red, Mediterranean, birch-associated, or other regional propolis types. This natural diversity means that colour, smell, stickiness, or one named compound cannot serve as a universal quality standard.

A professional propolis system therefore begins with source control. Dedicated propolis traps generally produce cleaner material than scraping random hive surfaces because scraped material can contain paint, wood fibres, hive treatments, old wax, dead insects, dirt, or residues from inappropriate construction materials.

This chapter focuses on safe collection, cleaning, storage, processing principles, quality characterisation, contamination control, realistic market uses, and the difference between biological research evidence and legally authorised health claims.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- explain where propolis comes from and how bees use it;
- describe why botanical and geographical origin strongly influence composition;
- distinguish clean trap-collected propolis from potentially contaminated scrapings;
- design a collection system that avoids paint, treated wood, pesticides, and hive-treatment contamination;
- clean, freeze, grade, store, and trace raw propolis;
- explain the principles of water-, ethanol-, and other solvent-based extraction without confusing extraction with medical efficacy;
- understand the importance of solvent identity, concentration, residues, and final product category;
- recognise allergy and contact-dermatitis risks;
- describe major laboratory approaches to propolis characterisation and adulteration detection;
- distinguish scientific evidence for biological activity from authorised consumer health claims;
- understand why food supplement, cosmetic, medicinal, and other product rules differ by jurisdiction.

## 56.1 What Is Propolis?

Propolis is a sticky resinous hive material produced when bees collect plant resins and mix them with wax and glandular secretions.

Its consistency changes strongly with temperature:

- cold propolis becomes hard and brittle;
- warm propolis becomes soft and tacky;
- at higher temperatures it becomes increasingly plastic and difficult to handle cleanly.

This temperature sensitivity is useful during collection and cleaning.

## 56.2 Plant Sources

Bees collect resin from sources such as:

- leaf buds;
- bark injuries;
- plant exudates;
- resinous leaf surfaces;
- flower-associated plant tissues in some regions.

Common source plants vary by region and can include species of *Populus*, *Betula*, *Baccharis*, *Clusia*, conifers, and many other resin-producing plants.

Do not assume that a propolis type from one country has the same chemistry as material from another.

## 56.3 Why Bees Collect Resin

Colonies use propolis to:

- seal cracks;
- reduce entrance gaps;
- reinforce hive surfaces;
- smooth rough cavities;
- fasten movable components;
- coat internal nest surfaces;
- encapsulate objects too large to remove.

In natural tree cavities, bees can build a continuous “propolis envelope” over much of the nest wall.

## 56.4 Social Immunity

Research suggests that propolis contributes to colony-level defence by modifying the microbial environment and influencing immune investment.

This is an example of social immunity: a colony uses collective behaviour and nest materials to reduce disease pressure.

It does not mean propolis prevents or cures every bee disease.

## 56.5 Colony Differences

Colonies differ in resin-collecting behaviour.

Variation can reflect:

- genetics;
- local resin availability;
- cavity/hive design;
- season;
- colony condition;
- environmental stress.

Some stocks propolise equipment heavily; others use little.

## 56.6 Seasonal Variation

Resin availability can change with:

- bud development;
- plant injury;
- temperature;
- rainfall;
- drought;
- agricultural activity.

Propolis harvested from the same apiary in different seasons can therefore differ chemically.

## 56.7 Chemical Diversity

Propolis can contain hundreds of identified compounds across different samples, including:

- flavonoids;
- phenolic acids;
- aromatic esters;
- terpenes;
- waxes;
- volatile compounds;
- plant-specific markers.

No one compound defines all propolis worldwide.

## 56.8 Propolis Types

Researchers often describe propolis by botanical/chemical type, such as:

- poplar-type propolis;
- Brazilian green propolis;
- Brazilian red propolis;
- Mediterranean propolis;
- Pacific or tropical resin types.

These categories support research and trade but require evidence rather than colour alone.

## 56.9 Colour

Raw propolis can be:

- yellow;
- green;
- red;
- brown;
- nearly black.

Colour reflects plant source, oxidation, wax, debris, and age.

A particular colour is not a universal purity grade.

## 56.10 Aroma

High-quality propolis often has a strong resinous, balsamic, plant-like aroma.

Foreign odours can indicate contamination from:

- fuel;
- smoke;
- solvents;
- paint;
- mould;
- inappropriate storage.

Sensory screening is useful but does not replace chemical analysis.

## 56.11 Why Random Scraping Is Risky

Scrapings from hive bodies can include:

- wood fibres;
- paint flakes;
- plastic fragments;
- old wax;
- bee parts;
- dirt;
- mould;
- metal corrosion products;
- veterinary-treatment residues;
- residues from treated timber.

For a commercial propolis product, collection method is part of quality control.

## 56.12 Dedicated Propolis Traps

Propolis traps are flexible grids or perforated sheets placed where bees are stimulated to fill small gaps with propolis.

Advantages include:

- cleaner collection surface;
- less wood/paint contamination;
- easier separation;
- standardised harvest area;
- improved traceability.

Traps must be made from materials suitable for the intended product use.

## 56.13 Trap Placement

Traps are commonly placed:

- above the top bars;
- beneath the hive cover;
- at other positions where airflow/light encourages sealing.

Effectiveness depends on colony behaviour, climate, and hive design.

Avoid creating excessive ventilation or weather exposure solely to force propolisation.

## 56.14 Collection Should Not Compromise the Colony

The goal is to harvest surplus propolis behaviour, not remove every protective resin layer from the nest.

Repeated aggressive scraping can:

- damage equipment;
- disrupt the propolis envelope;
- expose raw wood;
- mix old dirty residues into the product.

Use traps for planned production.

## 56.15 Trap Materials

A trap material should be:

- food-/product-contact suitable for the intended market;
- flexible enough for harvest where appropriate;
- resistant to cracking;
- free from objectionable odours;
- easy to clean and trace.

Unknown recycled plastic is not a suitable default for a premium food/supplement ingredient.

## 56.16 Freezing the Trap

Cold propolis becomes brittle.

A common physical harvesting method is to chill or freeze a filled flexible trap and flex it so propolis fragments release.

The important principle is temperature-assisted brittle separation, not one universal freezer duration.

## 56.17 Condensation After Freezing

Frozen traps can collect condensation when brought into warm humid air.

Water can:

- wet raw propolis;
- encourage surface contamination;
- complicate storage.

Handle frozen material in a clean dry workflow and allow equipment to equilibrate appropriately.

## 56.18 Scraped Propolis

Scraped propolis can still have value when collected from known clean, untreated surfaces.

Keep it separate from trap propolis and grade it according to:

- source surface;
- visible contamination;
- wax content;
- age;
- treatment history.

Do not blend low-grade scrapings into premium trap-collected propolis without disclosure and quality evaluation.

## 56.19 Painted Hives

Propolis touching painted external or internal surfaces can pick up paint fragments or chemicals.

Avoid harvesting product from painted interfaces unless the coating is specifically suitable and the collection method prevents contamination.

Internal hive surfaces generally should not be coated with inappropriate paint or preservatives in the first place.

## 56.20 Treated Timber

Wood preservatives can contaminate resin scraped from treated surfaces.

Do not collect propolis for human-use products from timber treated with unknown or unsuitable chemicals.

Source-control records should identify hive construction materials.

## 56.21 Veterinary Treatments

Some hive treatments can contaminate wax, propolis, or hive surfaces.

Before harvesting propolis for sale, review:

- active ingredient;
- product label;
- placement;
- treatment period;
- withdrawal or product restrictions;
- local residue requirements.

Do not assume a honey-super restriction is the only relevant product restriction.

## 56.22 Environmental Contaminants

Propolis can contain environmental contaminants such as:

- pesticides;
- heavy metals;
- combustion pollutants;
- dust.

Risk depends on apiary location and botanical source.

Premium suppliers may require residue or contaminant testing.

## 56.23 Collection Timing

Harvest timing depends on:

- trap fill level;
- seasonal resin flow;
- colony condition;
- weather;
- market demand.

Leaving traps indefinitely can increase dust, wax deposition, and mechanical damage.

## 56.24 Raw Propolis Handling

After collection:

1. protect material from floor contact;
2. remove obvious foreign bodies;
3. assign lot identity;
4. keep different apiaries or grades separate where required;
5. cool/freeze if part of the cleaning process;
6. package dry material promptly.

## 56.25 Manual Cleaning

Cold brittle propolis can be sorted to remove:

- wood;
- dead bees;
- large wax pieces;
- plastic;
- stones/dirt;
- other obvious debris.

Use clean tools and surfaces.

## 56.26 Sieving

Cold crushed propolis can be sieved by particle size.

Sieving can improve consistency but does not remove:

- dissolved pesticides;
- heavy metals;
- chemically similar adulterants.

Physical cleanliness and chemical purity are different questions.

## 56.27 Wax Content

Raw propolis naturally contains wax, but excessive wax can dilute resin-rich constituents.

Wax proportion depends on:

- colony behaviour;
- collection surface;
- scraping method;
- season;
- processing.

Commercial specifications may define acceptable wax or extractable-matter ranges.

## 56.28 Storage of Raw Propolis

Store raw propolis:

- clean;
- dry;
- cool;
- protected from light;
- sealed from odours;
- labelled by lot.

Long warm storage can alter volatile compounds and increase oxidation.

## 56.29 Refrigeration and Freezing

Cool or frozen storage can help preserve volatile and oxidation-sensitive constituents.

Before opening a frozen container, manage condensation by allowing sealed material to equilibrate where appropriate.

The ideal storage condition depends on intended shelf life and processing system.

## 56.30 Grinding

Cold propolis is easier to grind because brittleness reduces smearing.

Grinding increases surface area and can accelerate extraction, but also increases:

- oxygen exposure;
- dust generation;
- contamination opportunities.

Process only what can be protected promptly.

## 56.31 Dust and Occupational Exposure

Fine propolis particles can contact skin and respiratory surfaces.

Workers with sensitivity may develop dermatitis or other allergic symptoms.

Use appropriate hygiene and exposure controls, especially during grinding and sieving.

## 56.32 Propolis Allergy

Propolis is a recognised cause of allergic contact dermatitis in susceptible individuals.

Reactions are associated with resin constituents and can cross-react with related botanical substances in some people.

Consumer products should not be marketed as universally harmless because they are “natural”.

## 56.33 Bee-Product Allergy

People allergic to bee products, balsams, fragrances, or particular plant resins may be at increased risk of reactions.

Product labels and safety information must follow local requirements.

Serious or persistent allergic symptoms require medical attention.

## 56.34 Extraction Defined

Propolis extraction transfers selected soluble compounds from raw resin into a solvent or carrier.

Extraction changes the product from raw propolis to an extract with properties determined by:

- solvent;
- concentration;
- particle size;
- time;
- temperature;
- mixing;
- filtration;
- source propolis.

There is no single universal “propolis extract”.

## 56.35 Ethanol Extraction

Ethanol can dissolve many resinous phenolics and is widely used in propolis research and commercial products.

A professional system must control:

- food/pharmaceutical/cosmetic grade as appropriate;
- ethanol concentration;
- flammability;
- ventilation;
- extraction vessel compatibility;
- residual solvent;
- legal product category.

This handbook does not provide a universal medicinal tincture recipe.

## 56.36 Ethanol Fire Safety

Ethanol vapour is flammable.

Extraction requires:

- no ignition sources;
- appropriate ventilation;
- approved storage containers;
- spill control;
- compliance with fire and workplace rules.

Do not heat open ethanol mixtures over flames or improvised heaters.

## 56.37 Water Extraction

Water extracts a different range of propolis compounds from ethanol.

Water-based products can avoid alcohol but introduce new stability challenges because water supports microbial growth more readily than concentrated ethanol.

A water extract requires validated preservation, hygiene, and shelf-life controls.

## 56.38 Glycerol and Other Food/Cosmetic Solvents

Glycerol, propylene glycol, oils, and other carriers are used in some formulations.

Each changes:

- extraction selectivity;
- viscosity;
- microbial stability;
- legal status;
- sensory profile.

Only ingredients authorised and suitable for the intended product category should be used.

## 56.39 Solvent Choice Changes the Chemistry

Two extracts made from the same raw propolis but different solvents can contain very different chemical profiles.

Therefore, research results obtained with one extract should not automatically be attributed to every commercial propolis preparation.

## 56.40 Extraction Ratio

Commercial and research extracts may report ratios such as raw propolis mass relative to solvent volume or final dry extract.

These numbers are meaningful only when the method is clearly defined.

A label such as “30% propolis” can be ambiguous without knowing whether it refers to raw material, dry extract, or another basis.

## 56.41 Temperature During Extraction

Moderate temperature can increase mass transfer, but heat can alter volatile or sensitive compounds and increases solvent hazards.

Use controlled validated processing rather than assuming hotter extraction is better.

## 56.42 Extraction Time

Longer extraction can increase recovery up to a point, after which gains may become small while oxidation, contamination risk, or production time increases.

Commercial processes should validate efficiency rather than copying arbitrary durations.

## 56.43 Filtration of Extracts

After extraction, insoluble wax and debris are usually removed by filtration or settling.

Filter materials must be compatible with:

- solvent;
- intended product;
- particle size;
- food/cosmetic/pharmaceutical requirements.

## 56.44 Solvent Removal

Some manufacturers concentrate extracts by removing part or all of the solvent under controlled conditions.

This can require:

- vacuum equipment;
- solvent recovery;
- explosion/fire controls;
- residual-solvent testing;
- temperature control.

It is not a casual kitchen process for commercial production.

## 56.45 Dry Extracts

Dry propolis extracts may be produced by specialised systems such as spray drying, freeze drying, or carrier-assisted drying.

The carrier becomes part of the product formulation and must be declared where required.

Dry extract concentration should be standardised analytically rather than inferred from colour.

## 56.46 Standardisation

Because propolis varies naturally, commercial extracts can be standardised to defined markers or total classes such as:

- total phenolics;
- total flavonoids;
- selected botanical markers;
- dry extract content.

A marker is a quality-control tool, not proof that the product treats disease.

## 56.47 Total Phenolics

Total phenolic assays provide a broad estimate of reducing phenolic compounds.

Results depend on:

- method;
- reference standard;
- solvent;
- matrix.

Values from different methods should not be compared carelessly.

## 56.48 Total Flavonoids

Total flavonoid methods can help compare batches within a defined system.

They do not capture every bioactive constituent and can respond differently to different flavonoid structures.

Use consistent validated methods.

## 56.49 Chromatographic Fingerprinting

HPLC or LC-MS can profile specific phenolics and botanical markers.

This supports:

- batch comparison;
- botanical-type characterisation;
- adulteration investigation;
- research.

Reference material is essential for interpretation.

## 56.50 GC-MS

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry is useful for volatile and derivatised components.

It can help characterise:

- essential/volatile fractions;
- botanical origin;
- contamination;
- chemical differences among propolis types.

## 56.51 Spectroscopy

FTIR, NMR, UV-visible, and other spectroscopic methods can create chemical fingerprints.

Combined with chemometrics, they can help classify origin or detect adulteration.

Their accuracy depends on validated reference datasets.

## 56.52 Antioxidant Assays

Laboratory assays such as DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, and related tests estimate chemical antioxidant activity under specific conditions.

They are not direct measurements of clinical benefit in humans.

Do not convert an antioxidant assay into a disease-treatment claim.

## 56.53 Antimicrobial Assays

Propolis extracts can inhibit microorganisms in laboratory systems.

Results depend on:

- propolis type;
- extract method;
- concentration;
- organism;
- assay design.

An in-vitro zone of inhibition does not establish safe, effective human treatment.

## 56.54 Research Evidence Versus Health Claims

A responsible product description separates:

- chemical composition;
- laboratory activity;
- animal research;
- human clinical evidence;
- authorised health claims;
- authorised medicinal indications.

These evidence levels are not interchangeable.

## 56.55 Food Supplement Status

In many markets propolis is sold as a food supplement, but rules differ.

Requirements can include:

- ingredient safety;
- food-business registration;
- contaminant limits;
- labelling;
- permitted nutrition/health claims;
- dosage presentation.

A supplement is not automatically a medicine.

## 56.56 EU Health-Claim Context

In the European Union, nutrition and health claims on foods are governed by specific legislation and authorised claim lists.

A seller cannot simply convert promising research on propolis into an unapproved disease-prevention or treatment claim.

Medicinal claims can change the regulatory category of the product.

## 56.57 Cosmetics

Propolis is used in some:

- creams;
- balms;
- soaps;
- oral-care products;
- deodorising formulations.

Cosmetic products require safety assessment, compliant ingredients, appropriate manufacture, and permitted cosmetic claims.

Allergy risk remains relevant in topical use.

## 56.58 Medicinal Products

If a product is presented as treating or preventing disease, it may fall within medicinal-product law depending on jurisdiction.

That can require:

- authorisation;
- evidence of quality, safety, and efficacy;
- controlled manufacture;
- pharmacovigilance.

Beekeeping experience alone does not authorise medicinal marketing.

## 56.59 Oral-Care Products

Propolis appears in toothpastes, mouthwashes, sprays, and lozenges.

Formulation must consider:

- allergen risk;
- ethanol or other solvent;
- flavour;
- microbiological stability;
- legal claims;
- age restrictions where relevant.

## 56.60 Veterinary Products

Research also investigates propolis in animal health and hive-health applications.

A product intended to treat animal disease can fall under veterinary-medicine rules.

Do not use experimental propolis treatments to replace authorised bee-disease control.

## 56.61 Adulteration

Propolis can be adulterated or diluted with:

- excessive beeswax;
- plant resins not collected by bees;
- synthetic colourants;
- cheap fillers;
- undeclared extracts.

Authentication often requires chemical fingerprinting plus source traceability.

## 56.62 Plant Resin Versus Propolis

Plant resin collected directly by humans is not automatically propolis.

Propolis is a bee-processed hive material with a biological production history.

Substituting raw plant resin without disclosure is a product-identity issue.

## 56.63 Wax Dilution

Wax naturally occurs in propolis, but excessive addition can lower extractable resin compounds.

Commercial specifications can use:

- wax content;
- extractable matter;
- chemical markers;
- fingerprinting

to control dilution.

## 56.64 Heavy Metals

Propolis can reflect environmental metal exposure.

Premium or high-consumption products may be tested for regulated contaminants such as lead, cadmium, arsenic, or others depending on market.

Apiary siting is part of contaminant prevention.

## 56.65 Pesticide Residues

Because resins come from plant surfaces, propolis can contain environmental pesticides.

Residue testing is especially relevant when:

- apiaries are near intensive agriculture;
- material enters supplements or cosmetics;
- buyers specify residue-free limits.

## 56.66 Microbiological Quality

Raw propolis has biological activity but should not be assumed sterile.

Microbiological specifications may be needed for extracts or finished products, especially water-containing formulations.

Good hygiene remains necessary.

## 56.67 Moisture

Raw propolis is generally a low-moisture resinous material, but water can be introduced by:

- condensation;
- wet cleaning;
- poor storage;
- water extraction.

Unexpected moisture can affect microbial stability and handling.

## 56.68 Foreign Bodies

Quality control should exclude:

- glass;
- metal;
- plastic;
- wood;
- dead insects;
- stones;
- excessive hive debris.

Trap design and cold sorting reduce these risks.

## 56.69 Lot Definition

A propolis lot may be defined by:

- apiary;
- harvest period;
- trap type;
- botanical region;
- grade;
- extraction batch.

Larger blended lots require stronger mass-balance records.

## 56.70 Traceability

Record:

- apiary and location;
- harvest date;
- collection method;
- hive material/treatment history;
- raw mass;
- cleaning grade;
- storage condition;
- extraction batch;
- solvent/carrier lot where applicable;
- final product lot.

## 56.71 Supplier Approval

If buying propolis, request information on:

- origin;
- collection method;
- botanical type if claimed;
- contamination controls;
- residue testing;
- adulteration testing;
- storage history.

Untraceable resin should not automatically enter a premium branded product.

## 56.72 Certificate of Analysis

A propolis CoA can include:

- moisture;
- wax;
- extractable matter;
- phenolic/flavonoid markers;
- chromatographic fingerprint;
- microbiology;
- pesticides;
- heavy metals;
- residual solvents;
- lot identity.

Tests should match the product and risk.

## 56.73 Sensory Grading

Raw propolis can be graded by:

- colour;
- aroma;
- cleanliness;
- waxiness;
- visible debris;
- stickiness/brittleness at defined temperature.

Sensory grading does not replace botanical or chemical verification.

## 56.74 Botanical-Origin Claims

A claim such as “green propolis” or a named botanical propolis should be supported by:

- regional source;
- plant ecology;
- chemical markers;
- chromatographic/spectroscopic fingerprint;
- supplier documentation.

Colour alone is insufficient.

## 56.75 Geographical Claims

Geographical origin may be supported by traceability and chemical profile.

However, similar plant sources can occur across borders, so chemical composition alone may not identify a country with certainty.

Use documented chain of origin.

## 56.76 Storage of Extracts

Extract stability depends on:

- solvent;
- concentration;
- oxygen;
- light;
- temperature;
- container;
- microbial preservation.

Use product-specific stability data rather than assuming raw-propolis shelf life applies to the extract.

## 56.77 Containers

Containers must be compatible with the extract.

Ethanol can interact with some plastics and closures. Oils can absorb or extract packaging components. Water extracts can support microbes.

Packaging compatibility is part of formulation development.

## 56.78 Light Protection

Phenolic and volatile compounds can change under light exposure.

Amber glass or other light-protective packaging is often used for extracts, but actual packaging should be validated for the product.

## 56.79 Headspace and Oxidation

Large headspace increases oxygen availability and can accelerate oxidation of sensitive compounds.

Filling systems should balance practical expansion space with oxidation control.

## 56.80 Retained Samples

Keep retained raw and finished samples where appropriate.

They can support:

- complaint investigation;
- chemical fingerprint comparison;
- stability studies;
- supplier disputes;
- contaminant investigations.

## 56.81 Stability Studies

A commercial propolis product needs evidence that it remains within specification over its declared shelf life.

Monitor parameters relevant to the formulation, such as:

- appearance;
- odour;
- marker compounds;
- microbiology;
- phase separation;
- alcohol content;
- package integrity.

## 56.82 Scale-Up

A small jar extraction may not behave like a large tank because of differences in:

- mixing;
- temperature gradients;
- filtration;
- solvent handling;
- fire classification;
- cleaning.

Commercial scale requires engineering and workplace controls.

## 56.83 Worker Safety

Hazards can include:

- ethanol fire risk;
- solvent vapour;
- propolis dermatitis;
- grinding dust;
- hot processing surfaces;
- lifting;
- glass breakage.

Risk assessment should match the process.

## 56.84 Cleaning Equipment

Sticky resin can be difficult to clean.

Choose equipment surfaces and cleaning methods compatible with:

- food/cosmetic use;
- solvent safety;
- wastewater handling;
- worker exposure.

Do not use unapproved industrial solvents merely because they dissolve propolis well.

## 56.85 Waste Solvent

Solvent-containing waste must not be poured into drains or soil casually.

Follow local hazardous/flammable-waste requirements and recover solvent in professional systems where appropriate.

## 56.86 Decision Table

| Finding | Main concern | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Clean trap propolis from known hives | Premium raw-material candidate | Sort, label, store cool/dry and test to specification |
| Scraping with paint/wood fragments | Physical/chemical contamination | Downgrade or reject for human-use product |
| Strong fuel/solvent odour | Chemical contamination | Segregate and investigate; do not mask with extraction |
| High wax content | Dilution/collection-method issue | Grade separately; measure extractable matter if required |
| “Green propolis” claim based only on colour | Unsupported origin claim | Verify botanical/chemical markers and traceability |
| Water extract with no preservation study | Microbial shelf-life risk | Hold until formulation/stability validated |
| Skin rash in worker | Possible contact allergy | Stop exposure and seek appropriate medical/occupational advice |
| Laboratory antimicrobial activity | Research evidence | Do not convert automatically into disease-treatment marketing claim |

## 56.87 Practical Raw-Propolis Procedure

### Before collection

- choose clean dedicated traps;
- verify hive materials and treatment history;
- assign apiary/harvest identifiers;
- inspect colonies without stripping essential nest propolis.

### Harvest

1. remove filled trap hygienically;
2. protect from soil/dust;
3. chill/freeze as appropriate for brittle release;
4. flex/remove resin into clean food-compatible container;
5. control condensation;
6. keep grades/apiaries separate.

### Cleaning and storage

7. sort cold material;
8. remove visible foreign bodies;
9. sieve/grind only as required;
10. weigh and label;
11. store cool, dry, dark, and sealed;
12. retain sample/test according to market specification.

## 56.88 Practical Extraction-Control Framework

For any commercial extract, define:

- raw-propolis lot;
- solvent/carrier identity and grade;
- raw-to-solvent or dry-extract basis;
- extraction time and temperature;
- filtration method;
- residual-solvent or alcohol specification;
- microbiological controls;
- marker/standardisation target;
- package;
- shelf-life evidence;
- legally permitted claims.

This framework is more reliable than copying one internet tincture recipe.

## 56.89 Scientific Notes

### Propolis is chemically plural

“Propolis” describes a bee-made material, not one fixed chemical formula. Different plant communities can create products with different dominant phenolics, terpenes, and biological activities.

### Extraction is selective

Solvents differ in polarity. The chemical composition of an extract therefore reflects both the raw propolis and the extraction system.

### In-vitro activity is not clinical efficacy

Laboratory antimicrobial or antioxidant assays are useful screening tools but do not establish dose, absorption, safety, or treatment benefit in humans.

### Propolis and the colony microbiome

The propolis envelope appears to influence microbial exposure and immune activity in colonies, supporting the idea that nest architecture is part of social immunity.

### Authentication needs fingerprints

Because colour and aroma overlap among propolis types, robust origin/authenticity work increasingly uses chromatography, spectroscopy, chemometrics, and traceability together.

## Key Takeaways

- Propolis is a chemically variable bee-made resin, not one standard substance.
- Dedicated traps generally produce cleaner commercial material than random hive scrapings.
- Paint, treated timber, veterinary medicines, environmental pollutants, wax, and hive debris can contaminate propolis.
- Cold handling makes propolis brittle and easier to separate, but condensation must be controlled.
- Ethanol, water, glycerol, and other extraction systems produce chemically different extracts.
- Extraction strength or dark colour does not prove therapeutic potency.
- Propolis can cause allergic contact dermatitis and other hypersensitivity reactions in susceptible people.
- Laboratory antioxidant or antimicrobial activity is not equivalent to an authorised health or medicinal claim.
- Food-supplement, cosmetic, medicinal, and veterinary product rules differ and can change the legal category of the same raw material.
- Strong propolis quality control combines source traceability, clean collection, defined processing, contaminant testing, chemical fingerprinting, and truthful claims.

## Chapter Summary

Propolis is valuable precisely because it is biologically complex, but that same complexity makes quality control difficult. Bees collect different plant resins in different environments, so propolis from separate regions or seasons can differ greatly in chemical composition, colour, aroma, and laboratory activity.

The cleanest production strategy is to control collection at the hive. Dedicated traps reduce wood, paint, dirt, and old wax contamination and allow the beekeeper to define a traceable harvest lot. Cold handling makes the resin brittle for removal and sorting, while cool dark storage slows oxidation and volatile loss.

Commercial processing must then define the product. Ethanol, water, glycerol, oil, and other extraction systems select different compounds and create different stability and safety requirements. A water extract, for example, can require a very different microbial preservation strategy from a high-ethanol product. The same research result cannot be transferred automatically between extract types.

Finally, propolis marketing must remain evidence-based. Laboratory antimicrobial and antioxidant effects are scientifically interesting, but they do not automatically authorise disease-treatment claims. Propolis can also cause allergy. A high-quality product therefore combines chemical characterisation with contaminant control, safe formulation, regulatory compliance, and clear separation between scientific research and consumer medical claims.
