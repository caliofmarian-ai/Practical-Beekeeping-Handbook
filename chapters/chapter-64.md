# Chapter 64 — Online Sales

## Introduction

Online selling can turn a local apiary into a national or international food business, but it does not remove the obligations that apply to food, consumer contracts, privacy, claims, traceability, product safety, or fulfilment. A website is a sales channel, not a legal exception.

The most common online mistake is to focus on design and payment before product information and operations. A beautiful product page is useless if the mandatory food information is missing, origin is unclear, delivery destroys the product, stock is oversold, customer data are insecure, or a recall cannot identify who received an affected lot.

Under current EU food-information rules, prepacked food offered by distance selling must generally display the mandatory food information—apart from the minimum-durability/use-by date exception specified in Article 14—before the purchase is concluded. All mandatory particulars must then be available at delivery. Current Irish FSAI guidance also makes clear that online food sellers are responsible for the food information they provide and remain subject to food-business registration, food-safety management, traceability, and recall requirements.

Online sales therefore connect four systems:

1. a compliant product-information system;
2. a secure e-commerce and payment system;
3. a physical fulfilment and delivery system;
4. a customer-data and traceability system.

This chapter explains how to build those systems for honey and other hive products while preserving margin, product quality, and customer trust.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- choose between an own website, marketplace, social-commerce, and wholesale-order portal;
- design compliant product pages for prepacked foods;
- understand current EU distance-selling food-information requirements as a jurisdiction-specific example;
- manage honey origin, product naming, net quantity, claims, allergens, and storage information online;
- build a secure checkout and payment workflow;
- manage VAT/tax, invoices, consumer-contract information, refunds, and withdrawal rights according to current law;
- calculate shipping cost and choose minimum-order or free-shipping thresholds rationally;
- package glass jars, comb honey, pollen, royal jelly, and other products for transport safely;
- maintain cold chain for perishable hive products;
- synchronise inventory and prevent overselling;
- connect online orders to lot traceability and product recall;
- control personal data, email consent, cookies/analytics, cyber security, fraud, and account access;
- measure conversion, cart abandonment, AOV, fulfilment cost, returns, repeat rate, and contribution by channel;
- build an online store that the operation can actually fulfil profitably.

## 64.1 Online Is a Channel, Not a Business Model

An online shop does not answer:

- what product is sold;
- who the customer is;
- why the product is different;
- whether the margin is sufficient;
- whether the operation can fulfil it.

Those decisions belong to the business model and marketing strategy.

## 64.2 Main Online Channel Options

Common channels include:

- own e-commerce website;
- online marketplace;
- social-media shop or checkout;
- click-and-collect ordering;
- subscription/repeat-order platform;
- B2B wholesale portal.

Each has different fees, data access, control, and customer ownership.

## 64.3 Own Website

Advantages include:

- full brand control;
- customer relationship;
- flexible product information;
- better long-term first-party data;
- less dependence on marketplace rules.

Costs include:

- website platform;
- payment fees;
- maintenance;
- security;
- analytics;
- customer acquisition.

## 64.4 Marketplaces

Marketplaces can provide existing traffic and trust.

Costs can include:

- commission;
- listing fees;
- advertising;
- fulfilment rules;
- limited customer data;
- platform policy changes.

Calculate net contribution, not only sales volume.

## 64.5 Social Commerce

Social platforms can shorten the path from content to purchase.

Risks include:

- dependence on platform access;
- limited food-information presentation;
- account suspension;
- rapid changes to tools and fees.

Mandatory information still has to reach the customer appropriately.

## 64.6 B2B Ordering Portals

Wholesale portals can improve repeat ordering by retailers.

Useful features include:

- case sizes;
- wholesale price;
- minimum order;
- lead time;
- stock status;
- invoice terms;
- product data sheets.

## 64.7 Product Page Before Checkout

A food product page should be designed as part of the legal information system.

For prepacked foods in EU distance sales, mandatory food information—except the specific date-of-minimum-durability/use-by exception—must generally be available before the purchase is completed.

A customer should not have to buy the jar first to discover what it is.

## 64.8 Information at Delivery

All mandatory particulars must be available at delivery under the EU Article 14 framework.

This means the physical package and online listing must agree.

A correct webpage does not cure a wrong jar label.

## 64.9 Product Name

Use the correct legal/product identity.

Plain honey should not contain undeclared added ingredients.

If ingredients are added, the product should be named to reflect its real composition according to local law.

## 64.10 Honey Origin Online

Origin shown online should match the physical label and traceability records.

For EU blends under rules applying from 14 June 2026, origin information is more detailed than under the previous generic EU/non-EU system.

Do not display a simplified “Irish-style honey” page that conflicts with the actual country-origin declaration.

## 64.11 Floral-Origin Information

A floral claim online should be supported by the same evidence required for the product itself.

Website copy does not have a lower evidence standard than the jar.

## 64.12 Net Quantity

Show actual pack size clearly.

A photograph can make a small jar look large.

Include net quantity in a form customers can understand before purchase.

## 64.13 Ingredients and Allergens

Pure honey has no added ingredients.

Composite hive products can contain:

- nuts;
- spices;
- milk ingredients;
- pollen;
- propolis;
- other allergens or sensitising materials.

Provide legally required ingredient/allergen information before purchase.

## 64.14 Storage Instructions

Where storage affects safety or quality, provide appropriate instructions.

Examples include:

- frozen pollen;
- refrigerated/frozen royal jelly;
- creamed honey protected from heat;
- comb honey protected from high temperature.

## 64.15 Health Claims Online

A website is advertising.

Claims rules apply to:

- product titles;
- descriptions;
- blog posts linked to sales;
- testimonials;
- banners;
- influencer content.

Do not use online distance from the jar to make claims that would be prohibited on pack.

## 64.16 Search Snippets and Meta Text

Search-engine titles and descriptions are also commercial communications.

Avoid disease-treatment or misleading origin claims in SEO text.

## 64.17 Product Images

Show:

- actual jar/package;
- front label;
- texture/colour;
- pack size context;
- optional back label.

Images should not materially misrepresent colour, quantity, or ingredients.

## 64.18 Batch Variation

Seasonal honey can change colour naturally.

Online copy can explain that the current harvest may vary slightly from photography.

Do not hide a major product change behind a generic image.

## 64.19 Availability

Accurate stock status prevents:

- overselling;
- refunds;
- customer frustration;
- substitution pressure.

Synchronise online stock with actual release status.

## 64.20 HOLD Stock Must Not Be Sellable

A batch awaiting laboratory results or investigation should be excluded from sale inventory.

Inventory status should connect quality-control release to e-commerce availability.

## 64.21 Lot Assignment

Orders should be connected to the lot actually shipped.

Possible methods include:

- pick-list lot recording;
- barcode scanning;
- warehouse batch selection;
- order-management integration.

This matters during recall.

## 64.22 Recall From Online Sales

An online business can have an advantage: customer delivery records may identify who received a lot.

Use that responsibly and in compliance with privacy law.

A recall system should allow rapid extraction of affected orders.

## 64.23 Product Page Version Control

When a label or formulation changes, update the online product page.

Archive previous versions where useful for traceability.

A customer who bought last year's product should not be shown only this year's ingredients during an investigation.

## 64.24 Checkout Information

Before payment, customers should understand:

- product;
- total price;
- delivery charge;
- delivery method;
- seller identity;
- applicable contract/cancellation information;
- expected delivery timing.

Exact consumer-law particulars vary by jurisdiction.

## 64.25 Consumer Rights

EU consumer law gives strong information and withdrawal rights for distance contracts, with defined exceptions.

Food businesses should check the current rules for the specific product—especially perishable, personalised, opened/sealed, or time-sensitive goods—rather than writing one generic “no returns on food” rule without legal review.

## 64.26 Terms and Conditions

Terms can address:

- seller identity;
- order acceptance;
- payment;
- delivery;
- cancellation;
- returns/refunds;
- liability;
- governing law where appropriate.

Terms cannot remove mandatory consumer rights.

## 64.27 Privacy Notice

Explain how customer data are used for:

- fulfilment;
- payment;
- delivery;
- customer service;
- marketing where consent/legal basis permits;
- fraud prevention;
- tax/accounting records.

Collect only what is needed.

## 64.28 Payment Data

Avoid storing payment-card data unnecessarily.

Use reputable payment providers with appropriate security and compliance.

Tokenised payment systems can reduce direct handling of card data.

## 64.29 Account Security

Protect e-commerce administration with:

- MFA;
- strong unique passwords;
- role-based access;
- prompt removal of former staff;
- recovery procedures.

## 64.30 Plugin and Software Risk

Website extensions can create vulnerabilities.

Control:

- updates;
- trusted sources;
- backups;
- permissions;
- unused plugin removal.

## 64.31 Backups

Back up:

- product data;
- orders;
- customer records;
- website content;
- configuration.

Test restoration, not only backup creation.

## 64.32 Fraud Orders

Potential warning signs include:

- unusual order value;
- mismatched billing/shipping information;
- repeated failed payments;
- reshipping requests;
- suspicious bulk purchase.

Use payment-provider fraud tools and manual review for high-risk cases.

## 64.33 Account Takeover

Customer accounts can be compromised.

Do not expose unnecessary order or payment data, and support secure password reset.

## 64.34 Email Security

A compromised business email can enable invoice fraud, password reset, and customer phishing.

Use MFA and domain-authentication controls appropriate to the email system.

## 64.35 HTTPS

A modern e-commerce site should use encrypted HTTPS connections.

Security certificates are necessary but not sufficient; application security still matters.

## 64.36 Privacy and Marketing Consent

An order email address is collected primarily to fulfil the order.

Marketing use must follow applicable privacy and electronic-communications rules.

Maintain consent/opt-out records where required.

## 64.37 Cookies and Analytics

Analytics and advertising technologies can trigger consent and privacy obligations.

Use only tools that the business understands and can disclose/control.

## 64.38 Data Minimisation

Do not collect data merely because software offers a field.

Examples of unnecessary data can include:

- date of birth when no age restriction applies;
- detailed lifestyle information unrelated to fulfilment.

## 64.39 Shipping Economics

Calculate fulfilment cost per order including:

- box;
- internal protection;
- tape;
- label;
- labour;
- carrier charge;
- breakage allowance;
- payment fee;
- reship/refund rate.

## 64.40 Free Shipping Is Not Free

If the customer pays no visible delivery charge, the business still pays the carrier.

The cost must be covered by:

- product margin;
- minimum order;
- order threshold;
- promotional budget.

## 64.41 Free-Shipping Threshold

A threshold can encourage larger baskets.

Choose it by comparing:

- AOV;
- contribution margin;
- average shipping cost;
- expected conversion effect.

Do not copy a competitor's threshold blindly.

## 64.42 Minimum Order

A minimum order can protect economics when fulfilment cost per order is high.

It may reduce conversion for trial buyers.

Test actual behaviour.

## 64.43 Carrier Selection

Compare carriers on:

- cost;
- tracking;
- breakage;
- delivery time;
- remote-area fees;
- claim process;
- collection schedule.

## 64.44 Delivery Promise

Promise what operations can reliably achieve.

“Next day” marketing is harmful if orders routinely wait three days for packing.

## 64.45 Cut-Off Times

Order cut-off times should reflect:

- packing staffing;
- carrier collection;
- cold-chain product limits;
- weekend risk.

## 64.46 Glass Jar Shipping

Glass requires protection from:

- impact;
- jar-to-jar collision;
- lid damage;
- leakage.

Packaging should survive normal parcel handling without excessive material.

## 64.47 Drop Testing

Test packed parcels under realistic conditions.

Record:

- pack configuration;
- jar count;
- breakage;
- lid movement;
- leakage.

Repeat after changing jar or carton supplier.

## 64.48 Leak Control

Honey leakage can destroy the whole parcel and damage carrier equipment.

Use:

- sound closures;
- appropriate tamper seals;
- internal containment;
- upright packing where useful.

## 64.49 Comb Honey Shipping

Comb honey is sensitive to:

- heat;
- crushing;
- movement;
- leakage.

Use rigid support and avoid hot transport conditions that soften wax.

## 64.50 Creamed Honey Shipping

High temperature can damage crystal structure and cause separation.

Shipping design should consider seasonal heat exposure.

## 64.51 Frozen Pollen

Frozen pollen requires a validated cold-chain shipping method.

Do not offer national frozen delivery simply because a domestic freezer works at the packing site.

## 64.52 Royal Jelly

Royal jelly can require chilled or frozen transport according to product specification.

Use:

- insulated packaging;
- validated coolant;
- defined delivery time;
- temperature monitoring where justified.

## 64.53 Perishable Shipments Before Weekends

Avoid dispatch schedules that leave temperature-sensitive products sitting in depots during non-delivery days unless the packaging is validated for that duration.

## 64.54 Weather Disruption

Heatwaves, storms, and transport disruption can change shipment risk.

Delay dispatch when protecting the product is more important than preserving a promised shipping date.

## 64.55 International Shipping

Cross-border sales can involve:

- customs;
- food import rules;
- animal-product rules;
- VAT/sales tax;
- language;
- labelling;
- courier restrictions.

Check destination requirements before offering the country at checkout.

## 64.56 Northern Ireland / Great Britain / EU Complexity

Trade rules can differ across nearby jurisdictions.

A seller based in Ireland should verify current requirements for the actual destination rather than assuming all UK/EU shipments are identical.

## 64.57 Customs Commodity Information

Export systems can require:

- commodity codes;
- origin;
- value;
- product description.

Incorrect declarations can delay or return parcels.

## 64.58 Live Bees Are Different

Queens, nuclei, and packages are not ordinary food parcels.

They can be subject to:

- animal-health rules;
- movement certification;
- carrier restrictions;
- seasonal temperature constraints.

Use a separate fulfilment system.

## 64.59 Candles and Cosmetics Are Different Too

A store selling honey plus candles or cosmetics handles multiple legal/product categories.

Do not copy food information templates onto cosmetics or vice versa.

## 64.60 Online Marketplaces and Product Categories

Marketplace rules may impose additional requirements beyond law.

Failure to follow them can suspend listings even if the product itself is lawful.

## 64.61 Inventory Synchronisation

If the same stock sells online, at markets, and wholesale, update a single reliable inventory source where practical.

Overselling limited seasonal honey damages trust.

## 64.62 Reserved Stock

When an online order is paid/accepted, reserve the stock promptly so another channel cannot sell the same units.

## 64.63 Batch Expiry and FEFO

Fulfilment can use FEFO principles where lots have different best-before/internal review dates.

Do not always pick the easiest carton from the front.

## 64.64 Packing Station

A good packing station includes:

- released inventory;
- correct cartons;
- protection materials;
- verified scale;
- shipping labels;
- lot capture;
- clean surfaces.

## 64.65 Pick Accuracy

Common errors include:

- wrong jar size;
- wrong floral type;
- wrong quantity;
- wrong address;
- wrong lot.

Use a pick/pack check proportional to volume.

## 64.66 Barcode Systems

Barcodes can improve:

- SKU accuracy;
- inventory;
- lot capture;
- packing speed.

They do not replace physical label verification.

## 64.67 Order Status

Useful statuses include:

- paid;
- awaiting fulfilment;
- packed;
- shipped;
- delivered;
- held/problem;
- refunded.

## 64.68 Customer Notifications

Automated messages can include:

- order confirmation;
- dispatch;
- tracking;
- delivery exception.

Do not use transactional emails as hidden marketing without considering consent rules.

## 64.69 Customer Service

Publish clear contact routes.

Respond promptly to:

- damaged jars;
- non-delivery;
- wrong product;
- quality concern;
- allergy/information question.

## 64.70 Damaged Parcel Procedure

Ask for useful evidence such as:

- order number;
- photographs of outer and inner packaging;
- damaged product;
- carrier label.

Use the data to improve packaging, not only to process a refund.

## 64.71 Food-Safety Complaint

If a customer reports:

- foreign body;
- fermentation;
- chemical odour;
- illness;
- wrong allergen information,

treat it as a quality/food-safety investigation linked to the lot.

## 64.72 Returns

Returned food should not automatically re-enter saleable stock because storage and tampering history are outside your control.

Use a defined disposition process.

## 64.73 Refund Economics

Track the reason for refunds.

Repeated refunds can reveal:

- packaging failure;
- misleading product page;
- poor carrier;
- wrong product fit.

## 64.74 Conversion Rate

For an online store:

`conversion rate = orders ÷ qualified sessions/visits`

Define the denominator consistently.

## 64.75 Cart Abandonment

Abandonment can be caused by:

- shipping cost revealed late;
- slow checkout;
- lack of trust;
- required account creation;
- payment failure;
- unclear delivery.

Investigate before assuming the product is too expensive.

## 64.76 Average Order Value

AOV helps evaluate:

- bundles;
- free-shipping threshold;
- pack-size mix.

Use contribution margin as well as revenue.

## 64.77 Contribution per Order

A useful metric is:

`order revenue − product variable cost − fulfilment − payment fees − shipping subsidy − channel fees`

Advertising can then be evaluated against actual contribution.

## 64.78 Customer Acquisition Cost

Include paid media and acquisition labour where practical.

Do not scale advertising because revenue rises if acquisition cost consumes all contribution.

## 64.79 Repeat Purchase

Repeat customers often improve economics because acquisition cost is lower.

Track repeat behaviour by customer cohort and product.

## 64.80 Email List Value

Measure:

- delivered emails;
- clicks;
- purchases;
- unsubscribes;
- revenue/contribution.

List size alone is a vanity metric.

## 64.81 Subscription Churn

If running subscriptions, track cancellation/churn and reasons.

A high churn rate can reveal poor frequency or product mismatch.

## 64.82 Website Speed

Slow mobile pages reduce usability and conversion.

Compress images and avoid unnecessary scripts while preserving product clarity.

## 64.83 Mobile-First Use

Many customers shop on phones.

Test:

- font size;
- buttons;
- product info;
- origin table;
- checkout;
- address entry

on real mobile screens.

## 64.84 Accessibility

Use:

- text contrast;
- semantic headings;
- alt text;
- keyboard access;
- form labels;
- non-colour-only information.

Accessible design improves customer reach.

## 64.85 Search Engine Optimisation

SEO should support useful discovery through:

- accurate titles;
- local/origin pages;
- useful educational content;
- structured product information;
- fast pages.

Avoid deceptive keyword claims.

## 64.86 Analytics

Track only data the business will use.

Useful measures include:

- traffic source;
- conversion;
- AOV;
- product performance;
- repeat purchase.

Excess tracking increases privacy complexity without necessarily improving decisions.

## 64.87 Downtime

Online stores can fail.

Plan:

- monitoring;
- backups;
- payment-provider status;
- alternative contact/order method for important wholesale customers.

## 64.88 Platform Dependence

Do not rely entirely on one marketplace or social platform.

Own:

- domain;
- customer relationship where lawful;
- product records;
- email list with proper consent;
- brand assets.

## 64.89 Decision Table

| Observation | Likely issue | Response |
|---|---|---|
| High traffic, low conversion | Product information/trust/price/checkout issue | Analyse product page and checkout rather than buying more traffic |
| High cart abandonment after delivery shown | Shipping economics | Rework carrier/rates/threshold or communicate earlier |
| Frequent jar breakage | Packaging/carrier failure | Redesign and retest pack before more advertising |
| Orders exceed released stock | Inventory integration failure | Connect stock/quality status and reserve orders promptly |
| Customer cannot see mandatory info pre-purchase | Compliance failure | Rebuild product page before continuing sales |
| Frozen product arrives thawed | Cold-chain failure | Stop route, validate packaging/delivery process |
| High revenue but low contribution | Fulfilment/acquisition cost problem | Calculate contribution per order/channel |
| Marketing email sent without valid basis | Privacy/compliance risk | Correct consent/data process |

## 64.90 Practical Online-Store Build Sequence

1. Define products and destination markets.
2. Confirm food-business and tax status.
3. Define mandatory product information.
4. Build product data master.
5. Choose platform/payment provider.
6. Create accurate product pages.
7. Configure stock/lot control.
8. Design/test physical shipping packaging.
9. Define shipping rates and delivery promise.
10. Write consumer/privacy/returns information.
11. Secure admin accounts and backups.
12. Test full order from mobile device through delivery.
13. Launch to limited traffic.
14. Measure contribution, errors, breakage and repeat purchase before scaling ads.

## 64.92 Scientific and Management Notes

### Distance reduces sensory evidence

Online customers cannot taste, hold, or smell the product before purchase. Accurate images, origin evidence, product information, reviews, and brand trust therefore substitute for some in-person sensory cues.

### Fulfilment is part of marketing

A broken jar or late parcel changes brand perception more strongly than many advertising messages. Logistics is part of the customer experience.

### Online channels create better traceability potential

Digital orders can identify customer, product, and shipment, enabling more targeted recalls—provided lot capture is implemented correctly.

### Convenience has a measurable cost

Free shipping, subscriptions, and fast fulfilment can increase sales while reducing contribution. Channel growth should be evaluated economically, not only by order count.

## Key Takeaways

- Online food sales remain food sales; digital channels do not remove food-law obligations.
- In the EU, most mandatory information for prepacked foods must be available before a distance purchase is concluded, and all mandatory particulars must be available at delivery.
- Online origin, ingredient, allergen, and claims information must match the physical product.
- A compliant product page is only one part of the system; fulfilment, lot traceability, consumer rights, privacy, and cyber security matter too.
- Free shipping has a real cost and should be designed from contribution economics.
- Glass jars, comb honey, pollen, and royal jelly require different transport systems.
- Returned food should not automatically return to saleable stock.
- Lot capture can make online recalls more targeted and efficient.
- Conversion, AOV, contribution, acquisition cost, repeat purchase, breakage, and refunds are more useful than traffic alone.
- The best online store is one the physical operation can fulfil safely, profitably, and consistently.

## Chapter Summary

An online beekeeping store combines food information, e-commerce software, fulfilment, and traceability. The customer must receive accurate product information before purchase, the payment and personal-data systems must be secure, and the physical jar or hive product must survive delivery in the condition promised.

EU distance-selling rules make online product information a formal food-law issue rather than a marketing preference. Current Irish guidance also reminds online food sellers that registration, food-safety management, traceability, and recall obligations still apply. Product pages therefore need accurate identity, origin, quantity, ingredients/allergens where relevant, and lawful claims.

The second half of e-commerce is physical. Glass breakage, leakage, heat damage, cold-chain failure, overselling, and wrong-item picking can erase the benefit of successful advertising. Packaging needs real transport testing, inventory must respect quality-release status, and orders should be linked to lots when traceability requires it.

Finally, online growth should be measured through contribution and retention. More traffic is not success if shipping subsidy, advertising, breakage, and fulfilment consume the margin. A professional online channel scales only after the complete order-to-delivery system works reliably.
