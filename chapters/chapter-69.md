# Chapter 69 — Insurance

## Introduction

Insurance does not make a beekeeping business safe, legal, profitable, or well managed. It transfers defined financial risks to an insurer under a contract. Risks that are excluded, outside the territorial scope, below the excess, undeclared, uninsured, or caused by breaches of policy conditions may remain with the business.

A beekeeper can face losses from very different events:

- a visitor trips over equipment at a farmers market;
- a customer alleges injury from a contaminated product;
- a delivery van is damaged;
- an extractor motor fails during peak harvest;
- a fire destroys jars, labels, supers, and stored honey;
- a storm damages an apiary;
- stolen stock disappears before Christmas sales;
- a cyberattack locks the online shop and traceability records;
- a worker is injured lifting supers;
- a courier loses high-value pollen or royal jelly;
- a food recall creates disposal, customer, and business-interruption costs.

No single insurance policy automatically covers all of these.

A practical insurance programme begins with a risk register and then asks which risks should be:

1. prevented;
2. reduced;
3. retained by the business;
4. contractually transferred;
5. insured.

This chapter uses Irish examples where useful but does not assume that all listed cover is legally compulsory. In Ireland, public liability insurance is generally prudent rather than universally required by statute for every self-employed business, although venues, clients, markets, landlords, and public contracts may require it. Road vehicles, employment arrangements, mortgages/leases, and specific contracts can create separate compulsory or contractual insurance duties.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- distinguish risk management from risk transfer;
- identify the main insurance categories relevant to beekeeping businesses;
- distinguish public liability from product liability;
- understand why defective-product law matters even when insurance is not legally mandatory;
- understand employers', motor, property, stock, business interruption, equipment breakdown, transit, cyber, professional, and event risks;
- compare limits, sublimits, excesses, exclusions, territorial scope, and policy conditions;
- understand occurrence versus claims-made concepts where relevant;
- value seasonal stock and equipment accurately enough to avoid underinsurance;
- prepare evidence for claims;
- review insurance when products, markets, employees, vehicles, or turnover change;
- avoid treating insurance as a substitute for food safety, traceability, contracts, or legal compliance.

## 69.1 Insurance Starts With the Risk Register

List the events that could create serious financial loss.

For each risk record:

- event;
- cause;
- likely frequency;
- potential severity;
- existing controls;
- maximum credible loss;
- insurance currently held;
- uninsured gap;
- responsible person.

Do this before comparing premiums.

## 69.2 Risk Treatment Options

A business can:

### Avoid

Example: stop selling an unvalidated high-risk product.

### Reduce

Example: improve traceability and glass control.

### Retain

Example: accept small jar-breakage losses within operating costs.

### Transfer by contract

Example: courier accepts defined liability for goods in transit.

### Insure

Example: buy product-liability cover for defined claims.

Strong businesses use a mixture.

## 69.3 Insurance Is a Contract

The policy wording defines:

- insured entity;
- insured activity;
- period;
- territory;
- limits;
- excess;
- exclusions;
- conditions;
- notification duties;
- claims basis.

A one-page schedule is not the whole contract.

Read the wording and ask questions before relying on cover.

## 69.4 Named Insured

Check that the correct legal entity is insured.

Possible mismatches include:

- policy in personal name but trading through a company;
- old business name after incorporation;
- one partnership member omitted;
- separate online brand not included in the business description.

The premium can be paid perfectly and the policy still be misaligned.

## 69.5 Business Description

The insurer should understand the real operation.

Disclose activities such as:

- keeping bees;
- honey extraction/packing;
- farmers markets;
- online sales;
- wholesale;
- pollination services;
- queen/nucleus sales;
- propolis or pollen products;
- cosmetics;
- candles;
- bee-venom work;
- workshops/apiary visits.

A policy for “honey retail” may not automatically cover bee-removal contracting or cosmetics manufacturing.

## 69.6 Public Liability

Public liability typically addresses legal liability for injury to third parties or damage to third-party property arising from business activities, subject to policy terms.

Relevant scenarios include:

- customer slips at market stall;
- hive damages neighbouring property;
- apiary visitor is injured;
- falling canopy damages a vehicle;
- demonstration equipment injures attendee.

It does not automatically cover product claims.

## 69.7 Irish Example: Public Liability

Current Irish public guidance states that a self-employed business is not universally required by law to carry public liability insurance, but it is strongly advisable where the public interacts with the business.

Markets, landlords, clients, and contracts frequently require evidence of cover regardless of statutory minimum.

Always distinguish “not legally universal” from “commercially optional”.

## 69.8 Product Liability

Product liability addresses claims that a supplied product caused injury or property damage, subject to policy wording.

Examples can include allegations involving:

- glass fragments;
- chemical contamination;
- undeclared allergen in flavoured honey;
- contaminated cosmetic product;
- defective candle causing damage;
- unsafe packaged hive product.

Product liability is especially important once products leave your direct control.

## 69.9 Defective-Product Law

Insurance is relevant because product liability can exist even without proving ordinary negligence in every case.

Ireland's Liability for Defective Products Act 1991 implements strict/no-fault liability principles for defective products in defined circumstances, and the framework was extended to primary agricultural products.

This means “I was careful” is not always the end of legal analysis.

## 69.10 Product Liability Is Not Product Recall Cover

Standard product-liability insurance may pay for legal liability arising from injury/damage but may not automatically pay the costs of:

- retrieving product;
- destroying stock;
- customer notification;
- retailer fees;
- crisis consultancy;
- lost sales.

Separate product-recall cover or extensions may be needed.

## 69.11 Recall Insurance

Where available, recall cover can address defined recall expenses.

Ask whether it covers:

- accidental contamination;
- malicious tampering;
- government-mandated recall;
- third-party recall costs;
- replacement product;
- business interruption;
- rehabilitation/communications.

Read triggers carefully.

## 69.12 Employers' Liability

Employers' liability covers defined legal liability for employee injury or illness arising from work.

Risk areas include:

- heavy lifting;
- stings;
- hot wax;
- extractors;
- chemicals;
- vehicle loading;
- falls.

Whether insurance is legally compulsory and the required limits vary by jurisdiction and contract. Even where not universally statutory, it may be commercially expected or bundled with business cover.

## 69.13 Volunteers and Family Workers

Do not assume unpaid helpers are automatically covered.

Tell the insurer if the business uses:

- family members;
- volunteers;
- trainees;
- casual seasonal labour;
- subcontractors.

Policy definitions of employee can differ from tax or employment-law definitions.

## 69.14 Bona Fide Subcontractors

If contractors perform work, verify:

- their insurance;
- limit;
- activity;
- expiry;
- territorial scope.

Your policy may contain conditions requiring subcontractors to carry specified cover.

## 69.15 Commercial Motor

Vehicles used for business must have the correct motor-insurance use under local law and policy terms.

Tell the insurer about:

- hive transport;
- honey deliveries;
- towing trailers;
- employee drivers;
- business mileage;
- overnight goods/equipment.

Private-use cover may be inadequate for commercial activity.

## 69.16 Trailers

Trailer insurance/liability treatment varies.

Check:

- towing-vehicle policy;
- trailer value;
- theft cover;
- load cover;
- road legality;
- driver licence limits.

A trailer full of colonies creates both vehicle and livestock risk.

## 69.17 Goods in Transit

Motor insurance may cover the vehicle but not the full value of cargo.

Goods-in-transit cover can apply to:

- honey;
- pollen;
- royal jelly;
- jars;
- equipment;
- market stock.

Check temperature-sensitive goods and breakage exclusions.

## 69.18 Courier Liability

Courier compensation may be limited by contract and may exclude:

- glass;
- liquids;
- food;
- perishables;
- insufficient packaging.

Do not assume the courier will reimburse retail value.

## 69.19 Property Insurance

Property cover can include:

- buildings;
- honey room;
- storage sheds;
- office;
- fixtures.

Risks can include:

- fire;
- storm;
- flood;
- theft;
- escape of water;
- impact.

Coverage depends on location and exclusions.

## 69.20 Contents and Equipment

Contents can include:

- extractors;
- tanks;
- pumps;
- filling machines;
- scales;
- refrigerators/freezers;
- wax equipment;
- computers;
- market stalls.

Keep an asset register with replacement values.

## 69.21 Apiary Equipment

Ordinary business property policies may not automatically cover hives in remote apiaries.

Ask specifically about:

- hive theft;
- storm damage;
- vandalism;
- fire;
- livestock damage;
- flood;
- off-premises property.

## 69.22 Bees and Livestock Value

Cover for live colonies may be limited or specialised.

Define what loss means:

- death;
- theft;
- poisoning;
- disease destruction;
- queen loss;
- transit loss.

Many biological losses are not standard insured events.

## 69.23 Disease Losses

Do not assume insurance covers colony destruction required by disease authorities.

Check for exclusions involving:

- infectious disease;
- government order;
- pests;
- contamination;
- gradual deterioration.

Risk management and reserves remain essential.

## 69.24 Stock Insurance

Stock can include:

- packed honey;
- bulk honey;
- pollen;
- royal jelly;
- propolis;
- wax;
- jars;
- labels;
- gift packaging.

Value can change dramatically through the year.

## 69.25 Seasonal Stock Peak

A December gift-stock peak can be much larger than average inventory.

Tell the insurer if stock value varies seasonally.

Some policies allow a seasonal increase; others require a declared maximum.

## 69.26 Valuation Basis

Check whether stock is valued at:

- cost;
- replacement cost;
- selling price;
- another defined basis.

Do not assume expected retail margin is insured.

## 69.27 Underinsurance

If assets are insured below true replacement value, a policy may reduce claim payments under an average/underinsurance clause where applicable.

Update values after:

- equipment purchases;
- inflation;
- building improvements;
- business growth.

## 69.28 Replacement Cost

Replacement cost can exceed original purchase price.

An extractor bought used five years ago may cost much more to replace quickly during harvest.

Insure according to policy valuation basis, not sentimental memory of price paid.

## 69.29 Business Interruption

Property damage can stop trading after the physical repair cost is known.

Business-interruption insurance can address defined losses such as:

- lost gross profit/revenue;
- continuing fixed costs;
- temporary premises;
- extra expense.

It usually depends on an insured underlying event.

## 69.30 Indemnity Period

The indemnity period is the maximum time during which covered business-interruption loss is measured.

Ask how long it would really take to:

- replace specialist equipment;
- rebuild premises;
- regain retailer listings;
- produce a new harvest.

A 12-month period may be short for an annual biological crop.

## 69.31 Honey's Annual Production Cycle

A fire after harvest can destroy a year's inventory while the next full crop may be many months away.

This makes beekeeping different from businesses that can manufacture replacement stock tomorrow.

Business-interruption modelling should consider biological seasonality.

## 69.32 Equipment Breakdown

Mechanical/electrical breakdown cover can protect defined machinery beyond ordinary external-damage insurance.

Relevant equipment includes:

- extractor motors;
- pumps;
- cold rooms;
- freezers;
- filling machinery;
- boilers/heaters.

Wear and tear is often excluded.

## 69.33 Refrigeration Breakdown

For royal jelly, fresh pollen, or frozen products, refrigeration failure can destroy stock without a building fire.

Ask whether policy covers:

- machinery breakdown;
- temperature change;
- stock deterioration;
- power-supply interruption.

These can be separate extensions.

## 69.34 Utility Failure

External electricity failure may be excluded or covered only under specific conditions.

Business continuity should include backup arrangements even when insured.

## 69.35 Cyber Insurance

Online businesses can suffer:

- ransomware;
- data breach;
- payment fraud;
- website outage;
- phishing;
- lost traceability data.

Cyber insurance may cover defined response, restoration, legal, forensic, or notification costs.

It does not replace basic cybersecurity.

## 69.36 Cyber Policy Conditions

Insurers may require controls such as:

- multifactor authentication;
- backups;
- patching;
- access control;
- endpoint protection;
- incident plan.

If declared controls are not actually maintained, claims can become disputed.

## 69.37 Professional Indemnity

Professional indemnity can be relevant where the beekeeper sells advice or professional services such as:

- consultancy;
- training;
- pollination planning;
- apiary management advice;
- paid technical inspections.

It generally addresses financial loss from professional errors rather than physical product injury.

## 69.38 Workshops and Courses

Training events can create combined risks:

- public injury;
- professional advice;
- live-bee stings;
- property damage.

Tell the insurer if courses or apiary experiences are part of the business.

## 69.39 Bee-Removal Services

Removing swarms or colonies from buildings can involve:

- ladders;
- heights;
- structural damage;
- public liability;
- professional error;
- motor use.

Ordinary honey-producer cover may not include this activity.

## 69.40 Pollination Services

Pollination contracts can create losses from:

- transit;
- pesticide exposure;
- colony theft;
- failure to meet colony-strength specification;
- property damage.

Insurance should be reviewed alongside the pollination contract.

## 69.41 Market Stall Cover

Farmers markets may require proof of:

- public liability;
- product liability;
- specific limits;
- insurer details;
- policy dates.

Check organiser rules before paying a non-refundable stall fee.

## 69.42 Event-Specific Insurance

One-off fairs, festivals, demonstrations, or rented venues can require short-term endorsements or event cover.

Do not assume the annual policy automatically covers every event.

## 69.43 Premises Liability

If customers visit the farm or honey room, risks increase from:

- parking;
- uneven ground;
- animals;
- machinery;
- bees;
- slips;
- children.

Control the site first; insure the remaining exposure.

## 69.44 Landowner Requirements

Landowners may require the beekeeper to hold public liability and indemnify them for certain claims.

Read apiary-site agreements carefully.

An indemnity clause can transfer risk beyond what the insurance policy covers.

## 69.45 Indemnities

An indemnity is a contractual promise to compensate another party for defined loss.

Do not sign broad indemnities without checking whether the insurer accepts them.

Some policies exclude liability assumed only by contract.

## 69.46 Limit of Indemnity

The limit is the maximum the insurer pays under defined policy terms.

Check whether the limit applies:

- per claim;
- per event;
- in aggregate per year;
- separately for product liability;
- with sublimits.

A high headline limit can hide small sublimits.

## 69.47 Aggregate Limit

An aggregate limit is the maximum across multiple claims during the policy period.

Product liability often uses aggregate structures.

Several claims from one contaminated batch can consume the limit.

## 69.48 Sublimits

Sublimits can apply to:

- stock deterioration;
- cyber breach;
- product recall;
- theft from vehicle;
- temporary premises;
- legal costs.

Read them before estimating protection.

## 69.49 Excess/Deductible

The excess is the amount the insured bears before the insurer pays according to policy terms.

A high excess can reduce premium but makes frequent small claims uneconomic.

Budget for the excess as retained risk.

## 69.50 Exclusions

Common exclusions can involve:

- wear and tear;
- gradual deterioration;
- known defects;
- illegal acts;
- pollution;
- specific products;
- infectious disease;
- cyber events;
- contractual liability;
- unattended vehicle theft.

Exclusions vary widely.

## 69.51 Conditions and Warranties

Policies can require specific controls.

Examples:

- locked premises;
- alarm use;
- fire extinguishers;
- refrigeration alarms;
- documented food hygiene;
- subcontractor insurance;
- vehicle security.

A condition is operational, not decorative wording.

## 69.52 Territorial Limits

Check where the policy applies.

Important when:

- selling online abroad;
- attending foreign markets;
- exporting honey;
- providing pollination across borders;
- shipping products to North America or other higher-litigation markets.

## 69.53 Jurisdiction Clause

The policy may specify which courts/jurisdictions are covered.

International sales can create claims outside the expected territory.

Confirm before exporting.

## 69.54 Occurrence Policies

An occurrence policy generally responds based on when the insured event/injury occurred, subject to wording and reporting duties.

Public/product liability often uses occurrence-type structures, but never assume.

## 69.55 Claims-Made Policies

Claims-made cover typically responds to claims first made during the policy period and can depend on notification and retroactive dates.

Professional indemnity and some cyber covers commonly use this structure.

Continuous cover and run-off arrangements can matter.

## 69.56 Retroactive Date

A claims-made policy can exclude work performed before a stated retroactive date.

Check especially when changing insurer.

## 69.57 Run-Off Cover

If a claims-made service business stops trading, claims can arise later.

Run-off cover may be needed to protect against later claims arising from past work.

## 69.58 Disclosure

Provide accurate information to insurers.

Material changes can include:

- new products;
- new premises;
- increased turnover;
- employees;
- exports;
- new vehicles;
- public apiary tours;
- cosmetics;
- bee venom.

Do not wait until claim time to disclose a higher-risk activity.

## 69.59 Turnover Declarations

Premiums can be based partly on turnover.

Estimate honestly and update if growth materially changes exposure.

Online expansion can double product distribution even when hive count stays unchanged.

## 69.60 Product Categories

Tell insurers when moving from simple honey to:

- food supplements;
- cosmetics;
- candles;
- bee venom;
- training;
- imported goods.

Each can change underwriting.

## 69.61 Claims History

Insurers can ask about previous claims and incidents.

Keep a record of:

- date;
- type;
- reserve/payment;
- corrective action;
- current status.

Honest disclosure is essential.

## 69.62 Near Misses

Not every near miss is an insurance claim, but serious near misses reveal risk.

Examples:

- canopy nearly collapses;
- freezer alarm catches a temperature rise;
- glass breaks before filling begins.

Use them to improve controls and insurance review.

## 69.63 Evidence Before a Loss

Maintain:

- photographs of premises/equipment;
- serial numbers;
- invoices;
- stock counts;
- maintenance;
- backups;
- traceability;
- contracts.

Claims are easier when asset existence and value are documented before destruction.

## 69.64 Asset Register

For each major asset record:

- description;
- serial/ID;
- purchase date;
- purchase price;
- current replacement estimate;
- location;
- photo;
- finance/ownership status.

Update annually.

## 69.65 Stock Records

Stock records support claims after theft/fire.

Record:

- bulk honey;
- packed honey;
- other hive products;
- packaging;
- work in progress;
- seasonal gift stock.

Inventory systems serve both business and insurance purposes.

## 69.66 Incident Reporting

Policy wording often requires prompt notification of incidents that may give rise to a claim.

Do not wait for a formal lawsuit if the policy requires earlier notice.

Notify according to policy instructions.

## 69.67 Do Not Admit Liability Prematurely

After an incident:

- help injured people;
- secure the area;
- preserve evidence;
- record facts;
- notify insurer.

Avoid making legal admissions or settlements without insurer/professional guidance where policy terms restrict this.

## 69.68 Claims Evidence

Evidence can include:

- photographs;
- CCTV;
- witness contacts;
- lot records;
- invoices;
- medical/incident forms;
- damaged equipment;
- laboratory results;
- communications;
- police/fire reports.

Preserve originals.

## 69.69 Product Complaint Escalation

A complaint about a jar chip can become a product-liability incident.

The business should:

- identify lot;
- hold related stock;
- preserve sample;
- investigate;
- decide regulatory recall needs;
- notify insurer when policy requires.

Food safety action must not wait for insurance approval.

## 69.70 Recall and Insurance Coordination

In a food emergency:

1. protect consumers;
2. comply with authority obligations;
3. stop affected product;
4. notify insurer promptly;
5. preserve costs/evidence.

Insurance follows safety action; it does not control whether unsafe food is recalled.

## 69.71 Business Continuity

Insurance should be integrated with continuity planning.

Plan alternatives for:

- extractor failure;
- cold-storage failure;
- inaccessible apiary;
- vehicle loss;
- premises fire;
- cyber outage;
- key-person illness.

Some disruptions are faster to survive through pre-arranged alternatives than through claim payment.

## 69.72 Emergency Cash Reserve

Claims can take time and excesses remain payable.

Maintain cash or credit for:

- urgent replacement;
- product withdrawal;
- temporary storage;
- vehicle hire;
- payroll.

Insurance is not instant liquidity.

## 69.73 Self-Insurance

Self-insurance means deliberately retaining a risk financially.

It is appropriate for predictable small losses the business can absorb.

It is dangerous for catastrophic liabilities beyond available capital.

## 69.74 Deductible Strategy

A business with strong reserves may choose a higher excess for lower premium.

Compare:

- premium saving;
- expected claim frequency;
- cash reserve;
- worst-case multiple excesses.

## 69.75 Broker Versus Direct Purchase

A specialist broker can help with complex risks, but the business must still verify the policy.

Ask brokers/insurers in writing about unusual activities.

Keep responses.

## 69.76 Compare Like With Like

Do not compare insurance only by premium.

Compare:

- insured activities;
- limits;
- excess;
- product liability;
- stock values;
- interruption period;
- off-premises cover;
- territory;
- exclusions;
- claims basis.

The cheapest quotation may cover less.

## 69.77 Certificate of Insurance

Markets/customers may request a certificate.

Check that it shows:

- correct insured;
- relevant cover;
- limits;
- dates;
- insurer.

A certificate is summary evidence, not the full policy wording.

## 69.78 Renewal Review

Do not auto-renew blindly.

Review:

- turnover;
- hive count;
- stock peak;
- new products;
- export countries;
- employees;
- vehicles;
- equipment;
- claims;
- new contracts;
- cyber systems.

## 69.79 Mid-Term Changes

Notify insurer when material changes occur during the year.

Examples:

- launching cosmetics;
- moving premises;
- hiring first employee;
- exporting to a new territory;
- installing large ethanol propolis process;
- opening farm tours.

## 69.80 Cancellation and Lapses

A lapse can create uninsured periods and problems for claims-made policies.

Track:

- renewal date;
- payment;
- certificate availability;
- replacement cover start.

Do not let annual market insurance expire during peak sales.

## 69.81 Claims-Made Continuity

When changing claims-made insurers, review:

- retroactive date;
- known circumstances;
- prior notification;
- run-off;
- continuity wording.

A cheaper new policy can create a historical gap.

## 69.82 Contract Review Before Insurance Review

Before signing a large wholesale/pollination/event contract, send relevant insurance clauses to the insurer/broker if necessary.

The contract may require:

- higher limits;
- indemnity to principal;
- additional insured status;
- special territories.

Do not sign first and ask later.

## 69.83 Insurance and Food Safety

Insurance cannot replace:

- HACCP;
- clean equipment;
- legal medicines;
- traceability;
- recall planning;
- correct labels.

Poor controls can increase claims and may breach policy conditions.

## 69.84 Insurance and Biosecurity

Insurance also does not replace:

- Varroa management;
- disease control;
- quarantine;
- movement records.

Many colony losses are operational risks the beekeeper must manage directly.

## 69.85 Insurance and Security

Insurers may expect reasonable protection against theft.

Controls can include:

- locks;
- alarms;
- secure vehicles;
- CCTV;
- hive marking/GPS where appropriate;
- inventory.

Never endanger yourself confronting thieves.

## 69.87 Annual Insurance Procedure

1. Update risk register.
2. Update asset register and replacement costs.
3. Calculate peak stock value.
4. Update turnover by product/channel/territory.
5. List employees/contractors/volunteers.
6. List vehicles and trailers.
7. List new products/services.
8. Review major contracts.
9. Compare current limits/exclusions with exposure.
10. Obtain quotations/endorsements.
11. Document decisions and uninsured retained risks.
12. Store policy, schedule, wording, certificate, broker contact, and claims procedure securely.

## 69.88 Claim Response Procedure

1. Protect life and prevent further damage.
2. Meet regulatory/food-safety obligations immediately.
3. Preserve evidence.
4. Record facts and witnesses.
5. Identify affected assets/products/lots.
6. Notify insurer/broker under policy timing.
7. Avoid unauthorised admissions/settlements.
8. Keep invoices for emergency costs.
9. Cooperate with investigation.
10. Review controls after the claim.

## Management Notes

### Insurance pricing is not the same as risk

A low premium may reflect a narrow policy rather than low exposure. Compare coverage architecture before price.

### Biological businesses need longer continuity thinking

A lost honey crop cannot be manufactured instantly. Indemnity periods and emergency reserves should reflect annual production cycles.

### Contract risk can exceed tort risk

A broad indemnity signed in a retailer or landowner contract can create obligations the business would not otherwise have. Insurance and contract review should be linked.

### Documentation is part of insurance value

Accurate stock, traceability, equipment, and maintenance records can speed claim assessment and reduce disputes.

### Prevention remains economically superior

The best claim is often the one prevented by food safety, maintenance, biosecurity, cybersecurity, and safe work design.

## Key Takeaways

- Insurance transfers defined financial risks; it does not eliminate hazards.
- Start with a risk register, not a premium comparison website.
- Public liability and product liability cover different exposures.
- Product-liability insurance may not cover recall expenses automatically.
- Property, equipment, stock, interruption, motor, transit, cyber, and professional risks may require separate sections or extensions.
- Seasonal stock and biological production cycles make valuation especially important in beekeeping.
- Limits, sublimits, excesses, exclusions, conditions, territory, and claims basis matter as much as headline cover.
- Material business changes should be disclosed during the policy period.
- Claims require prompt safety action, evidence preservation, and policy notification.
- Insurance never replaces food safety, traceability, legal compliance, or business continuity.

## Chapter Summary

Beekeeping insurance should be designed around the real business rather than purchased as a generic certificate. A direct-market honey producer, a queen breeder, a pollination contractor, an online pollen retailer, and a farm-tour operator expose insurers to different risks even if each keeps 100 colonies.

Public liability addresses third-party injury/property damage from business activity, while product liability addresses defective-product claims. Property, stock, commercial motor, goods in transit, equipment breakdown, business interruption, cyber, professional indemnity, and specialist recall cover address different financial consequences.

The policy contract matters in detail. Limits can be per claim or aggregate, sublimits can reduce important sections, exclusions can remove disease or deterioration, and claims-made policies can create retroactive-date and continuity issues. A certificate proves only a summary of cover.

The business should review insurance whenever turnover, products, employees, markets, contracts, premises, vehicles, or territories change. The strongest insurance programme sits on top of good prevention: safe food, safe work, secure data, maintained equipment, precise traceability, and realistic continuity planning.
