# Writing Standards

Project: Practical Beekeeping Handbook

Version: 1.1

---

# Mission

The goal of this handbook is to provide one of the most practical, accurate, and accessible beekeeping references available in English.

The handbook shall combine scientific knowledge, practical field experience, modern beekeeping techniques, and original educational illustrations to create a resource suitable for both beginners and experienced beekeepers.

---

# Target Audience

This handbook is intended for:

- Beginners
- Hobby beekeepers
- Commercial beekeepers
- Students
- Teachers
- Researchers
- Anyone interested in honey bees and modern apiculture

Technical depth may vary by chapter, but essential procedures must remain understandable to a careful beginner.

---

# Language Standard

The handbook shall use clear international English with British English spelling as the editorial default.

Examples:

- behaviour, not behavior
- organisation, not organization
- litre, not liter
- centre, not center

Scientific names, quotations, official titles, product names, and legal terminology must retain their recognised or original spelling.

Writers must not mix British and American spelling within the same chapter.

---

# Voice and Tone

The handbook shall use a professional, calm, practical, and evidence-aware tone.

Writers should:

- Address the reader directly when giving practical instructions.
- Prefer active voice where it improves clarity.
- Use third person for scientific descriptions where appropriate.
- Avoid conversational filler.
- Avoid fear-based language.
- Avoid exaggerated promises.
- Avoid presenting personal preference as universal practice.
- Explain why an action is recommended whenever that reason helps the reader make a decision.

Instructions must be respectful and must not assume that the reader owns expensive equipment, manages large numbers of colonies, or works in one particular country.

---

# Writing Style

All handbook content shall follow these rules:

- Use clear international English.
- Prefer short, direct sentences.
- Keep most paragraphs focused on one main idea.
- Explain technical terms when they first appear.
- Avoid unnecessary jargon.
- Avoid exaggerated or promotional claims.
- Separate verified facts from practical recommendations.
- State uncertainty when evidence is limited.
- Use consistent terminology throughout the handbook.
- Write for practical use in the apiary, not only for theory.
- Prefer precise verbs over vague expressions.
- Avoid unnecessary repetition.
- Do not use rhetorical questions as a substitute for explanation.
- Do not describe a procedure as easy, simple, safe, or guaranteed unless that description is justified.

---

# Controlled Terminology

The same concept must use the same preferred term throughout the handbook.

Preferred examples include:

- colony for the complete social unit of bees
- hive for the physical structure housing a colony
- brood for eggs, larvae, and pupae collectively
- brood frame for a frame containing brood
- super for a box intended primarily for honey storage
- apiary for a managed location containing one or more colonies
- beekeeper for the person managing colonies
- queenright for a colony confirmed or reasonably assessed to have a functioning queen
- queenless for a colony lacking a functioning queen

Colloquial, regional, or alternative terms may be mentioned when useful, but the preferred term must remain dominant.

Scientific names must be italicised where Markdown rendering permits it, for example *Apis mellifera* and *Varroa destructor*.

The glossary must be consulted before introducing a new technical term.

# Required Chapter Structure

Each chapter should contain, where relevant:

1. Chapter title
2. Brief chapter introduction
3. Learning objectives
4. Main explanation
5. Practical application
6. Step-by-step procedures
7. Decision points
8. Practical tips
9. Common mistakes
10. Safety warnings
11. Scientific notes
12. Tables, diagrams, illustrations, or figure placeholders
13. Summary
14. Key takeaways
15. Practical checklist
16. References

A section may be omitted only when it does not serve the subject. Omission must not leave the chapter without practical guidance, safety information, or evidence where these are relevant.

---

# Markdown Heading Structure

Each manuscript file must contain one level-one heading only.

Use headings in this order:

- `#` Chapter title
- `##` Major section
- `###` Subsection
- `####` Minor subsection, used sparingly

Do not skip heading levels.

Do not use bold text as a substitute for a heading.

Headings should be descriptive, concise, and written in title case.

Repeated generic headings such as “Other Information” or “More Details” should be avoided.

---

# Paragraphs, Lists, and Procedures

Paragraphs should normally be concise and readable on both mobile screens and printed pages.

Use bullet lists for:

- unordered items;
- symptoms;
- equipment;
- options;
- advantages and disadvantages;
- inspection observations.

Use numbered lists only when order matters.

Step-by-step procedures must:

1. State the purpose.
2. Identify required equipment or conditions.
3. Present actions in the correct order.
4. Include decision points where outcomes may differ.
5. Include safety warnings before the risky step.
6. State what successful completion looks like.
7. Explain what to do when the expected result is not observed.

Each procedural step should begin with a clear action verb.

---

# Practical Focus

Every chapter must answer the question:

> How can the reader apply this information in a real apiary?

The handbook should include concrete procedures, decision points, warning signs, examples, and field-ready checklists whenever possible.

Practical recommendations must distinguish between:

- essential actions;
- preferred practices;
- optional methods;
- emergency measures;
- practices that depend on local conditions.

Recommendations must not imply that one hive type, management system, treatment, or seasonal schedule is universally correct.

---

# Regional and Seasonal Context

Beekeeping practice varies with climate, forage, bee population, hive type, law, and local disease pressure.

Statements affected by location or season must identify the relevant limitation.

Use wording such as:

- In temperate climates...
- During a strong nectar flow...
- Where local regulations permit...
- Depending on colony strength...
- Consult the competent authority in your country or region...

Do not assign fixed calendar dates to biological events unless the statement is clearly identified as a regional example.

Seasonal guidance should prioritise observable colony and environmental conditions over calendar dates.

---

# Measurements and Units

Use metric units as the primary standard.

Examples:

- millimetres: mm
- centimetres: cm
- metres: m
- grams: g
- kilograms: kg
- millilitres: mL
- litres: L
- degrees Celsius: °C

Where useful for an international audience, imperial equivalents may follow in parentheses.

Example:

- 25 kg (55 lb)

Use a non-breaking conceptual space between a number and its unit in final publication formatting.

Do not alternate between units without a practical reason.

Measurements must not imply false precision. Use ranges when biological or field variation is expected.

---

# Numbers, Dates, Time, and Temperature

Spell out numbers from one to nine in ordinary prose unless they accompany a unit, measurement, age, percentage, chapter reference, table, or procedure.

Use numerals for 10 and above.

Examples:

- three colonies
- 5 kg of stores
- 12 frames
- 8%
- Chapter 4

Use unambiguous dates in the format:

- 26 July 2026

Use the 24-hour clock where exact times are necessary.

Temperature must be stated primarily in degrees Celsius.

Approximate values must be identified as approximate.

---

# Safety Labels

Safety information must use consistent labels.

Use:

- **Note:** Useful information that prevents confusion.
- **Caution:** A practice that may cause manageable harm, damage, contamination, or colony disturbance.
- **Warning:** A credible risk of serious injury, severe allergic reaction, fire, poisoning, major colony loss, or legal breach.
- **Emergency:** Immediate action is required to protect life or prevent severe harm.

Place the label before the risky action, not after it.

Warnings must be specific. State:

- the hazard;
- who or what is at risk;
- the preventive action;
- the response if the event occurs.

Do not overuse warning labels, because excessive warnings reduce their effectiveness.

---

# Safety Rules

Whenever a subject involves risk, the chapter must include a clearly marked safety section.

Relevant risks may include:

- Bee stings
- Severe allergic reactions
- Smoke and fire
- Sharp tools
- Heavy hive components
- Chemical treatments
- Food contamination
- Infectious bee diseases
- Transport and public safety
- Heat stress
- Working alone
- Unsafe lifting
- Children, visitors, livestock, and neighbours

Safety information must be practical, specific, and proportionate to the actual risk.

Medical guidance must not diagnose the reader. Emergency symptoms must direct the reader to appropriate emergency medical assistance.

Treatment instructions must never encourage use contrary to the authorised product label or applicable law.

---

# Accuracy and Evidence

Scientific, medical, legal, regulatory, and food-safety claims must be supported by reliable sources.

Preferred sources include:

- Peer-reviewed scientific papers
- Systematic reviews
- Universities
- Government agencies
- Official legislation
- Recognised bee-health institutes
- Professional beekeeping organisations
- Authoritative veterinary and food-safety bodies

Unverified claims, folklore, and unsupported remedies must not be presented as established fact.

Anecdotal practices may be discussed only when clearly identified as anecdotal, traditional, experimental, or insufficiently verified.

Claims must not be made stronger than the supporting evidence.

The difference between correlation, plausible mechanism, field observation, and demonstrated causation must be respected.

---

# Citations and References

All citations and reference lists must follow `docs/standards/CITATION_GUIDE.md`.

Citations are required for:

- scientific claims;
- disease biology;
- treatment efficacy;
- pesticide and veterinary medicine information;
- medical information;
- food-safety requirements;
- legal or regulatory claims;
- numerical thresholds derived from research or official guidance;
- disputed or rapidly changing subjects.

Common practical instructions do not require a citation in every sentence, but the scientific or regulatory basis of the recommendation must be traceable.

Sources must be checked directly. Do not cite a source solely because another source cites it.

Do not invent titles, authors, dates, identifiers, quotations, or links.

---

# Legal and Regulatory Content

Legal requirements vary between jurisdictions and may change.

Legal sections must:

- identify the relevant jurisdiction;
- state the date or version checked where practical;
- cite the official source;
- avoid presenting general information as personalised legal advice;
- instruct readers to verify current local requirements.

The handbook must not imply that registration, treatment, food labelling, transport, sales, import, export, or disease-reporting requirements are identical across countries.

---

# Originality

All text, diagrams, tables, and educational explanations must be original or properly licensed.

The handbook must not copy substantial passages from other books, articles, websites, standards, or training materials.

Sources should be paraphrased accurately and cited properly.

Changing a few words does not make copied structure or wording original.

Direct quotations should be rare, brief, necessary, and properly attributed.

AI-assisted drafts must be reviewed for accuracy, originality, fabricated references, repetition, and unnatural wording before acceptance.

---

# Visual Elements

Figures, diagrams, photographs, and tables must:

- Support the text directly
- Have a clear purpose
- Use consistent numbering
- Include a descriptive caption
- Be referenced in the chapter text
- Use original or properly licensed material
- Remain understandable in print and digital formats
- Avoid decorative content that does not improve understanding

Numbering examples:

- Figure 4.2
- Diagram 7.1
- Table 9.3
- Checklist 5.1

Visual content must follow the dedicated illustration and table standards.

---

# Figure Placeholders

Until an approved visual is available, use a plain placeholder in the manuscript.

Format:

`[Figure placeholder: concise description of the required visual]`

Example:

`[Figure placeholder: labelled cross-section of a Langstroth hive showing the floor, brood boxes, queen excluder, supers, crownboard, and roof]`

A placeholder must describe:

- the subject;
- the educational purpose;
- essential labels or viewpoints;
- any safety-relevant details.

Do not use ASCII art as a substitute for a planned figure.

---

# Tables and Checklists

Tables must be used only when they improve comparison, decision-making, or field reference.

Checklists must:

- use observable or verifiable items;
- avoid vague entries;
- follow the order in which the reader should work;
- distinguish mandatory checks from optional observations where relevant;
- fit the intended field task.

Tables and checklists must not duplicate long passages of nearby text without adding practical value.

---

# Cross-References and Duplication Control

Before adding a detailed explanation, writers must check whether the subject is already covered elsewhere.

Use cross-references when another chapter contains the primary explanation.

Example:

> For full instructions on assessing queen status, see Chapter 27, Requeening.

A chapter must remain understandable on its own, but entire procedures should not be copied into multiple chapters.

When brief repetition is necessary for safety or immediate decision-making, keep it concise and refer to the full treatment elsewhere.

Chapter numbers and titles must be verified against `docs/BOOK_OUTLINE.md` before publication.

---

# Accessibility and Inclusiveness

Instructions must not rely only on colour where shape, position, text, or pattern can also communicate the information.

Captions and visual descriptions should make the educational purpose understandable to readers who cannot view every detail of the image.

Avoid assumptions about the reader's physical strength, sex, age, nationality, income, or access to specialised equipment.

Where heavy lifting is involved, include safer alternatives such as smaller boxes, mechanical aids, or team lifting.

---

# Quality Standard

Each chapter should be strong enough to function as a useful standalone practical guide while remaining consistent with the rest of the handbook.

Before a chapter is marked complete, it must be checked for:

- Accuracy
- Clarity
- Practical value
- Consistent terminology
- Safety
- References
- Originality
- Visual support
- Grammar
- Formatting
- Regional limitations
- Legal limitations
- Unit consistency
- Cross-reference accuracy
- Duplication
- Accessibility

---

# Editorial Completion Checklist

A chapter may be marked complete only when all applicable statements below are true:

- [ ] The chapter follows the official outline.
- [ ] Learning objectives match the chapter content.
- [ ] Technical terms are defined at first use.
- [ ] Practical instructions are complete and correctly ordered.
- [ ] Decision points and failure responses are included.
- [ ] Safety information appears before hazardous actions.
- [ ] Scientific and regulatory claims have reliable citations.
- [ ] Regional and seasonal limitations are stated.
- [ ] Measurements and terminology are consistent.
- [ ] Tables, checklists, and visuals add practical value.
- [ ] Figure placeholders follow the approved format.
- [ ] Cross-references are correct.
- [ ] Unnecessary repetition has been removed.
- [ ] The text has been checked for originality.
- [ ] Grammar, spelling, Markdown, and formatting have been reviewed.
- [ ] References are complete and traceable.
