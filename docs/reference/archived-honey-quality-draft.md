# Chapter 52 — Honey Quality

## Introduction

Honey quality cannot be reduced to one number. A jar can have acceptable moisture but false origin. It can have excellent flavour but excessive veterinary residues. It can be authentic and safe but outside a customer's colour specification. It can comply with a legal compositional standard yet have suffered enough warm storage to lose premium aroma and enzyme activity.

A complete quality assessment therefore asks four different questions:

1. **Is it legally and compositionally honey?**
2. **Is it safe and hygienically acceptable?**
3. **Is the declared botanical and geographical identity truthful?**
4. **Does it meet the intended sensory and commercial specification?**

These questions overlap but require different evidence.

Major international and European standards use measurements such as moisture, reducing sugars, sucrose, free acidity, electrical conductivity, diastase activity, and hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF). Authenticity and origin may require additional tools such as pollen analysis, stable-isotope methods, chromatographic profiles, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), DNA-based methods, and traceability records.

No simple home “purity test” replaces these systems.

## Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- distinguish legal compliance, safety, authenticity, origin, and sensory quality;
- explain the practical meaning of moisture, water activity, sugars, sucrose, acidity, electrical conductivity, diastase, and HMF;
- understand the major current Codex and EU compositional criteria and their exceptions;
- recognise fermentation, overheating, contamination, and foreign-body defects;
- explain how sensory assessment contributes to quality control;
- understand pollen analysis and its limitations;
- describe major laboratory approaches to detecting sugar adulteration and origin fraud;
- take representative honey samples and interpret certificates of analysis;
- connect quality results to harvest, processing, and storage records;
- avoid unsupported authenticity claims based on folklore tests.

## 52.1 Quality Has Several Dimensions

A useful quality framework includes:

- composition;
- freshness/processing history;
- sensory quality;
- microbiological stability;
- chemical safety;
- physical cleanliness;
- authenticity;
- botanical origin;
- geographical origin;
- traceability;
- packaging integrity.

One test rarely addresses more than one or two dimensions.

## 52.2 Legal Standard Versus Premium Specification

A legal standard defines minimum or maximum criteria for marketability under that law.

A premium buyer may require stricter values for:

- moisture;
- HMF;
- colour;
- flavour;
- pollen spectrum;
- residue status;
- crystallisation;
- country or apiary origin.

Meeting the law does not automatically mean meeting every buyer's specification.

## 52.3 Codex and EU Standards

The Codex *Standard for Honey* provides an international reference. The European Union uses Directive 2001/110/EC as amended, with a consolidated version applying from 14 June 2026.

Values are similar in many areas but legal application depends on the market where the honey is sold.

Always use the current applicable standard rather than copying a table from an old book.

## 52.4 Moisture

Moisture content strongly influences:

- fermentation risk;
- viscosity;
- crystallisation behaviour;
- storage stability;
- mass balance.

It is commonly measured by refractometry.

Representative sampling and calibration are as important as instrument resolution.

## 52.5 General Moisture Criterion

Under current EU criteria, honey in general is limited to not more than 20% moisture, with specified exceptions such as heather and certain baker's honey categories.

Codex also uses a general 20% maximum for most honey.

A legal maximum is not necessarily the preferred commercial target for long storage.

## 52.6 Water Activity

Water activity measures available water rather than total water mass.

It can help assess microbial stability, especially in unusual or borderline products.

Moisture and water activity correlate but are not interchangeable.

## 52.7 Fructose and Glucose

Fructose and glucose are the dominant sugars in most honey.

Current EU criteria require minimum combined fructose plus glucose values that differ broadly between:

- blossom honey;
- honeydew honey and blends involving honeydew.

These criteria help describe normal honey composition but do not alone prove authenticity.

## 52.8 Sucrose

Sucrose is normally present at relatively low levels because nectar sucrose is enzymatically transformed during honey ripening.

High sucrose can reflect:

- botanical origin;
- immature harvest;
- feeding contamination;
- adulteration;
- analytical variation.

The general EU maximum is 5 g/100 g, with named botanical exceptions that permit higher values.

Do not diagnose adulteration from sucrose alone without considering floral source.

## 52.9 Other Sugars

Honey can contain many oligosaccharides and minor sugars.

Sugar profiles can support:

- botanical characterisation;
- authenticity studies;
- fermentation investigation;
- process research.

They are usually measured chromatographically in laboratories.

## 52.10 Refractive Solids and Brix

Refractometers may display Brix or honey moisture.

Brix is a soluble-solids scale originally based on sucrose solutions. Honey is a complex mixture, so honey-specific refractive tables are preferable when the objective is moisture.

Do not convert blindly between scales without the correct method.

## 52.11 Free Acidity

Free acidity reflects titratable organic acids in honey.

It differs from pH:

- pH measures hydrogen-ion activity;
- free acidity measures the quantity of base required to titrate acids to a defined endpoint.

The two values provide related but different information.

## 52.12 Current Free-Acidity Criterion

Under current EU rules, free acidity is generally not more than 50 milliequivalents of acid per 1,000 g, with a higher allowance for baker's honey.

Elevated acidity can be associated with fermentation or natural composition and must be interpreted in context.

## 52.13 pH

Honey is naturally acidic.

pH influences:

- flavour;
- enzyme activity;
- microbial ecology;
- chemical reactions;
- HMF formation.

pH alone is not a standard authenticity test.

## 52.14 Electrical Conductivity

Electrical conductivity reflects the concentration of ions, minerals, organic acids, and related constituents.

It is useful in distinguishing many blossom honeys from honeydew honeys and some dark mineral-rich honeys.

## 52.15 Current Conductivity Criterion

Under current EU criteria:

- most honeys and their blends are not more than 0.8 mS/cm;
- honeydew honey, chestnut honey, and their blends generally are not less than 0.8 mS/cm;
- named botanical exceptions exist.

Conductivity therefore supports classification but must be combined with origin knowledge.

## 52.16 Ash and Minerals

Older standards often used ash content as a broad mineral measure.

Electrical conductivity largely replaced ash as a routine classification tool because it is faster and correlates well with mineral/acid content.

Mineral analysis remains useful for research, contamination, and origin studies.

## 52.17 Diastase

Diastase activity measures amylase enzyme activity.

It can fall during:

- heating;
- prolonged storage.

Natural starting levels differ among floral sources.

## 52.18 Current Diastase Criterion

The current EU general criterion is at least 8 on the Schade scale.

Honey with naturally low enzyme content can have a lower minimum of 3 Schade units when its HMF is correspondingly very low under the defined exception.

This paired interpretation prevents a naturally low-enzyme honey from being judged solely by one number.

## 52.19 HMF

HMF is formed from sugar degradation.

Its level tends to increase with:

- heat;
- time;
- acidity;
- storage temperature.

Fresh honey normally begins with low HMF.

## 52.20 Current HMF Criterion

Under current EU criteria, HMF is generally not more than 40 mg/kg after processing and blending, except for honey of declared origin from tropical-climate regions and blends of these honeys, where a higher limit applies.

Codex uses a similar approach.

A processor should preserve low HMF rather than use the legal maximum as a target.

## 52.21 HMF Is Not an Authenticity Test by Itself

High HMF can suggest heat or age, but low HMF does not prove:

- floral origin;
- geographical origin;
- absence of sugar syrup;
- absence of residues.

Each quality question requires appropriate evidence.

## 52.22 Diastase and HMF Together

The combination is more informative than either value alone.

Examples:

- low HMF + strong diastase: consistent with fresh, gently handled honey;
- rising HMF + falling diastase: compatible with heat/age exposure;
- naturally low diastase + very low HMF: may fit a recognised floral exception.

These are interpretations, not mathematical proof of exact history.

## 52.23 Water-Insoluble Matter

Standards limit water-insoluble solids to reduce excessive foreign material.

Possible insoluble matter includes:

- wax;
- insect fragments;
- plant debris;
- dust.

Pressed honey can have a different limit in some standards because of its production method.

## 52.24 Sensory Quality

Sensory assessment evaluates:

- aroma;
- flavour;
- aftertaste;
- mouthfeel;
- texture;
- colour appearance;
- defects.

A trained panel can detect changes that routine chemical tests do not capture fully.

## 52.25 Sensory Defects

Common defects include:

- smoke taint;
- fermentation;
- burnt/caramelised notes;
- chemical odour;
- stale aroma;
- metallic taint;
- foreign flavours;
- excessive foam or debris.

Sensory detection should trigger investigation, not be hidden by blending.

## 52.26 Floral Aroma

Volatile compounds contribute strongly to varietal character.

Excessive heat or long open-tank exposure can reduce these compounds even while basic legal composition remains acceptable.

Premium quality therefore often requires stricter processing than legal minimums.

## 52.27 Colour

Honey colour is influenced by:

- floral origin;
- minerals;
- phenolic compounds;
- storage;
- heating;
- blending.

Colour can be measured on systems such as the Pfund scale.

Colour classification is a commercial descriptor, not a universal measure of nutritional or sensory superiority.

## 52.28 Pfund Scale

The Pfund system expresses colour from very pale to dark using a defined optical comparison.

It can help:

- grade lots;
- blend consistently;
- meet buyer specifications.

It does not determine floral origin alone.

## 52.29 Crystallisation Quality

Crystallisation can be:

- desirable in creamed honey;
- acceptable in naturally crystallised honey;
- undesirable in a product sold as clear liquid honey.

Quality depends on the intended product specification.

## 52.30 Phase Separation

Partly crystallised honey can separate into liquid and crystal-rich phases.

This can:

- create unattractive texture;
- redistribute water;
- increase fermentation risk in the liquid phase.

Measure and investigate rather than simply reheating repeatedly.

## 52.31 Fermentation Quality

Fermentation indicates yeast activity under suitable moisture conditions.

Indicators include:

- gas;
- pressure;
- alcoholic/sour aroma;
- frothing;
- flavour change.

Fermented honey generally does not meet ordinary table-honey quality expectations and may fall into a different legal or industrial-use category depending on jurisdiction.

## 52.32 Yeast and Mould Counts

Honey can contain yeasts and mould spores despite low water activity.

Laboratory counts are especially useful when investigating:

- fermentation;
- high-moisture lots;
- shelf-life problems;
- sanitation failures.

Microbial count interpretation depends on method and product condition.

## 52.33 Honey Is Not Sterile

Natural honey can contain microbial spores.

Of particular public-health importance, honey can contain *Clostridium botulinum* spores. For this reason, public-health guidance advises against feeding honey to infants under 12 months.

Routine honey processing does not sterilise these spores reliably.

## 52.34 Veterinary Medicine Residues

Residue monitoring may include substances associated with:

- Varroa treatments;
- antibiotics where misused or legally used under restricted systems;
- environmental contamination.

Compliance depends on:

- authorised use;
- maximum residue limits or action limits;
- market requirements;
- analytical capability.

## 52.35 Pesticide Residues

Honey can contain residues from environmental pesticide exposure.

Risk assessment considers:

- compound;
- concentration;
- legal maximum residue level where applicable;
- exposure route;
- market.

One residue detection does not automatically prove misuse by the beekeeper.

## 52.36 Heavy Metals and Elements

Honey can accumulate trace elements from:

- soil and plants;
- industrial pollution;
- processing equipment;
- inappropriate containers.

Analytical panels may include lead and other elements depending on regulatory and buyer requirements.

Use suitable food-contact materials to avoid preventable contamination.

## 52.37 Smoke and Combustion Contaminants

Improper smoker fuel, fires, heaters, and vehicle exhaust can introduce off-odours or combustion contaminants.

Food-quality prevention begins in the apiary and continues through transport and processing.

## 52.38 Cleaning-Chemical Residues

Detergents and sanitisers can contaminate honey through:

- incorrect concentration;
- poor rinsing;
- wet equipment;
- accidental chemical transfer.

Chemical taint is a process-control failure.

## 52.39 Foreign Bodies

Physical quality defects include:

- glass;
- metal;
- plastic;
- insects;
- wood;
- hairs;
- excessive wax.

Prevention uses equipment maintenance, straining, housekeeping, packaging control, and glass policies.

## 52.40 Authenticity

Authenticity asks whether the product is truly what it claims to be.

Fraud can involve:

- addition of exogenous sugar syrups;
- false botanical origin;
- false geographical origin;
- false country declaration;
- dilution;
- substitution;
- misrepresentation of processing or product category.

## 52.41 Sugar-Syrup Adulteration

Adulterants can include syrups derived from:

- maize/corn;
- sugar cane;
- beet;
- rice;
- wheat;
- other starch or sugar sources.

Modern syrups can be formulated to mimic simple honey sugar ratios, so routine sugar analysis alone may not detect every fraud.

## 52.42 Stable-Isotope Analysis

Carbon stable-isotope ratio analysis can detect some additions of sugars from C4 plants such as cane and maize.

Limitations include:

- C3 syrups can be harder to detect by classical C4 methods;
- natural botanical variation;
- need for appropriate reference proteins/controls;
- fraud methods evolve.

No one isotope test detects every adulterant.

## 52.43 LC-IRMS and Advanced Isotope Methods

Liquid chromatography–isotope ratio mass spectrometry can separate individual sugars before isotope analysis and improve detection of some adulteration patterns.

These methods require specialised laboratories and validated interpretation.

## 52.44 Nuclear Magnetic Resonance

NMR creates a broad chemical fingerprint.

It can support assessment of:

- authenticity;
- botanical origin;
- geographical origin;
- unusual composition.

Its power depends heavily on high-quality reference databases.

## 52.45 Chromatography and Mass Spectrometry

GC-MS and LC-MS can profile:

- volatile compounds;
- phenolics;
- sugars;
- residues;
- adulteration markers.

Non-targeted profiling can detect unusual patterns but requires statistical validation.

## 52.46 DNA-Based Methods

DNA metabarcoding and targeted DNA methods can detect biological material from:

- plants;
- bees;
- microorganisms.

They may complement pollen microscopy in origin studies.

DNA abundance does not translate directly into nectar percentage, so interpretation requires care.

## 52.47 Pollen Analysis

Melissopalynology examines pollen in honey.

It can support:

- botanical-origin assessment;
- geographical clues;
- detection of unusual source patterns.

Pollen representation depends on plant biology, bee behaviour, extraction, filtration, and analytical preparation.

## 52.48 Under- and Over-Represented Pollen

Some nectar plants produce pollen that appears in honey disproportionately high or low relative to nectar contribution.

Therefore, a numerical pollen percentage cannot always be read directly as the percentage of nectar from that plant.

Expert interpretation uses botanical knowledge and other product characteristics.

## 52.49 Sensory and Microscopic Evidence Together

A varietal honey claim can be stronger when multiple evidence types agree:

- pollen spectrum;
- flavour/aroma;
- colour;
- conductivity;
- sugar profile;
- known apiary forage;
- harvest timing.

No single characteristic should be forced to carry the entire claim.

## 52.50 Geographical Origin

Geographical origin can be supported by combinations of:

- traceability;
- pollen assemblages;
- elemental patterns;
- isotope ratios;
- chemical fingerprints;
- DNA profiles;
- documentation.

Analytical origin determination is probabilistic and reference-database dependent.

## 52.51 EU Origin Labelling From 14 June 2026

Under Directive (EU) 2024/1438 and national transposition, EU honey blend labels generally require countries of origin to be shown in descending order by weight with percentage shares, subject to defined tolerances and limited options.

This makes processing mass balance and supplier traceability part of consumer-facing quality.

## 52.52 Traceability as Authenticity Evidence

Laboratory testing is stronger when supported by documented chain of custody.

Useful records include:

- apiary;
- harvest date;
- country;
- supplier lot;
- blend weight;
- tank;
- packing lot;
- laboratory sample ID.

A laboratory result without clear sample identity is weakened evidence.

## 52.53 Certificate of Analysis

A certificate of analysis (CoA) should contain enough information to understand what was tested.

Review:

- laboratory identity;
- sample/lot identity;
- date;
- method;
- units;
- result;
- uncertainty where relevant;
- specification or reference limit;
- accreditation scope where required.

## 52.54 Accredited Laboratories

Accreditation such as ISO/IEC 17025 can demonstrate laboratory competence for defined methods within its scope.

It does not mean every test offered by the laboratory is necessarily accredited.

Check the current scope for the relevant method.

## 52.55 Sampling Is Often the Weakest Link

A perfect laboratory cannot correct an unrepresentative sample.

Sampling errors include:

- choosing only the top of a separated tank;
- sampling one drum from a heterogeneous shipment;
- contaminated utensils;
- mislabelling;
- mixing source lots accidentally;
- insufficient sample mass.

## 52.56 Representative Sampling

A sampling plan should define:

- lot boundaries;
- number of containers;
- random or risk-based selection;
- composite versus individual samples;
- mixing requirements;
- sample volume;
- container;
- storage/transport;
- chain of custody.

## 52.57 Retained Samples

A retained sample can demonstrate what the producer held at a specific stage.

Use tamper-evident or controlled storage where appropriate and record:

- lot;
- date;
- sample point;
- storage condition;
- person collecting.

## 52.58 Measurement Uncertainty

All analytical measurements have uncertainty.

When a result lies close to a legal or contract limit, interpretation should consider:

- method uncertainty;
- repeatability;
- sample uncertainty;
- decision rules used by the laboratory or authority.

A displayed value with many decimal places is not automatically more certain.

## 52.59 Reference Methods

Standards and laboratories specify validated methods because different methods can produce slightly different results.

Use recognised methods for regulatory or commercial decisions.

Home instruments are valuable for process control but may not replace accredited laboratory testing in disputes.

## 52.60 Moisture Quality Control

A producer can use a calibrated refractometer routinely and send periodic or disputed samples to a laboratory.

Good records include:

- instrument ID;
- calibration/check date;
- sample temperature;
- result;
- lot.

Trend data can reveal harvest or storage problems early.

## 52.61 HMF Quality Control

HMF is usually measured in a laboratory using validated spectrophotometric or chromatographic methods.

Use it to evaluate:

- heat exposure;
- storage history;
- incoming bulk honey;
- shelf-life changes.

Do not attempt to judge HMF from colour alone.

## 52.62 Diastase Quality Control

Diastase analysis uses a defined enzymatic method and is expressed in units such as the Schade scale.

Results should be interpreted alongside:

- HMF;
- botanical source;
- storage history;
- processing record.

## 52.63 Conductivity Quality Control

Conductivity can be measured relatively easily with calibrated equipment and correctly prepared solutions.

It can support classification but is not a standalone origin certificate.

Follow the reference method exactly for comparable results.

## 52.64 Acidity Quality Control

Free acidity is typically measured by titration.

Unexpectedly high results should trigger review of:

- fermentation;
- floral origin;
- storage;
- sampling;
- laboratory method.

## 52.65 Sugar Analysis

High-performance liquid chromatography is commonly used to quantify sugars.

Sugar profiles help assess:

- compositional compliance;
- botanical characteristics;
- potential adulteration;
- maturation.

Interpretation must account for authorised botanical exceptions.

## 52.66 Residue Analysis

Residue methods can reach very low detection limits.

A detected trace and a legal violation are not automatically the same.

Compare results with:

- legal MRL/action threshold;
- uncertainty;
- authorised use;
- market requirement.

## 52.67 Non-Detects

“Not detected” means the analyte was not found above the method's detection/reporting capability under the tested conditions.

It does not prove absolute zero.

Reports should state or make available detection/quantification limits where relevant.

## 52.68 Microbiological Testing

Routine honey safety does not always require the same microbiological programme as high-water foods.

Testing may be targeted toward:

- osmophilic yeasts;
- moulds;
- specific spore surveillance;
- process investigations.

The quality plan should match actual risks.

## 52.69 Sensory Panel Design

A sensory panel should control:

- sample temperature;
- coded containers;
- serving order;
- palate cleansing;
- lighting where colour bias matters;
- vocabulary;
- reference standards.

Informal tasting is useful for beekeepers but differs from formal sensory evaluation.

## 52.70 Defect Investigation

When a defect is found:

1. hold the lot;
2. define the defect objectively;
3. review process/storage history;
4. compare retained sample;
5. test appropriate parameters;
6. identify affected scope;
7. decide release, rework, downgrade, or rejection.

Do not blend first and investigate later.

## 52.71 Out-of-Specification Results

An OOS result should trigger a documented investigation.

Possible causes include:

- true product failure;
- sampling error;
- laboratory error;
- calculation/transcription error;
- wrong specification;
- lot mix-up.

Retesting should follow a defined procedure rather than repeating tests until a passing number appears.

## 52.72 Adulteration Suspected

If incoming honey is suspected of adulteration:

- quarantine the lot;
- preserve samples;
- stop blending;
- review supplier documentation;
- use competent authenticity testing;
- document decisions;
- notify authorities or customers where legally required.

Do not attempt to “correct” fraudulent honey by adding genuine honey.

## 52.73 False Floral Claims

A floral claim can fail even when the honey itself is genuine.

For example, honey can be authentic but falsely sold as a premium monofloral variety.

Authenticity therefore includes truthful description, not merely absence of syrup.

## 52.74 False Geographical Claims

Origin fraud can occur when honey from one country or region is labelled as another.

Strong prevention includes:

- approved suppliers;
- shipping records;
- country-of-origin documents;
- mass balance;
- analytical verification for high-risk claims.

## 52.75 Organic and Other Certification Claims

Organic, protected geographical, monofloral, raw, local, or other marketing claims may have additional legal or certification requirements.

Do not infer certification from good beekeeping practice alone.

Use the governing standard for each claim.

## 52.76 “Raw Honey”

“Raw” is not defined identically in every jurisdiction.

Consumers may associate it with minimal heating and processing, but legal definitions vary or may be absent.

A producer should make only claims that can be supported and are permitted locally.

## 52.77 “Pure Honey”

All product legally sold simply as honey should meet the definition of honey without unauthorised additions.

“Pure” should not imply that competing legally compliant honey is somehow impure without evidence.

Avoid misleading marketing.

## 52.78 Home Authenticity Myths

Tests based on:

- whether honey burns;
- whether it dissolves in water;
- whether it wets paper;
- whether ants visit it;
- whether it crystallises;
- how it behaves on a thumb

cannot reliably determine authenticity.

Different genuine honeys behave differently because of moisture, floral source, temperature, and crystals.

## 52.79 Quality Decision Table

| Result/observation | What it can indicate | What it does not prove alone |
|---|---|---|
| High moisture | Greater fermentation risk/non-compliance | Adulteration |
| High HMF | Heat/age exposure | Exact heating temperature or syrup addition |
| Low diastase | Natural source or heat/age | Fraud by itself |
| High conductivity | Honeydew/chestnut or other mineral-rich origin possible | Exact botanical source |
| Positive pollen profile | Botanical/geographical support | Exact nectar percentage without interpretation |
| Stable-isotope anomaly | Possible exogenous sugar addition | Every possible adulteration route |
| Fermentation odour | Yeast spoilage likely | Chemical contamination identity |
| Trace pesticide detection | Environmental or treatment exposure | Legal violation without comparison to limit |

## 52.80 Practical Quality-Control Programme

### Incoming/harvest

- define lot;
- measure moisture;
- verify treatment/origin records;
- inspect sensory condition.

### Processing

- control time–temperature;
- preserve lot identity;
- prevent water/chemical contamination;
- document blend weights.

### Finished bulk/pack

- sample representatively;
- test parameters required by law/customer;
- retain sample;
- release only after review.

### Ongoing

- monitor complaints;
- audit suppliers;
- review trends;
- update specifications.

## 52.81 Quality Specification Example Structure

A specification can include:

- product name/category;
- country/botanical claim;
- moisture;
- HMF;
- diastase;
- conductivity;
- acidity;
- sugar profile;
- colour;
- sensory profile;
- residue requirements;
- authenticity requirements;
- packaging;
- shelf life.

Values must come from applicable law and commercial objectives.

## 52.82 Scientific Notes

### Quality markers are indirect

HMF, diastase, conductivity, pollen, and isotopes each provide evidence about particular aspects of honey. None is a universal truth detector.

### Reference databases matter

Non-targeted authenticity tools such as NMR or metabolomics compare a sample against known genuine profiles. Poor or unrepresentative reference data can weaken classification.

### Botanical variability is real

Natural honey composition varies with plant species, geography, weather, and season. Quality control must distinguish natural variability from fraud.

### Fraud evolves with testing

As analytical methods improve, adulteration strategies can change. Robust authenticity programmes use multiple orthogonal methods and traceability rather than one permanent test.

### Sampling uncertainty can exceed analytical uncertainty

A laboratory may measure one vial very precisely, but if the vial does not represent the 20-tonne lot, the result can still mislead. Sampling design is therefore part of analytical science.

- [ ] Moisture measured correctly.
- [ ] HMF and diastase tested when required.
- [ ] Conductivity/acidity/sugar profile interpreted with botanical context.
- [ ] Sensory condition reviewed.
- [ ] Fermentation signs absent or investigated.
- [ ] Residue programme matches market risk.
- [ ] Foreign-body controls verified.

## Key Takeaways

- Honey quality is multidimensional: composition, safety, authenticity, origin, sensory quality, and traceability are different questions.
- Legal limits are not automatically premium-quality targets.
- Moisture influences fermentation, but water activity provides a more direct view of microbial water availability.
- HMF and diastase help assess heat/storage history but do not prove authenticity alone.
- Electrical conductivity supports honey classification but has botanical exceptions.
- Crystallisation is natural; phase separation and fermentation are different phenomena.
- Pollen analysis is powerful but requires expert interpretation because pollen can be under- or over-represented.
- Modern authenticity testing uses validated laboratory methods and reference databases; no home purity test can replace them.
- EU origin rules applying from 14 June 2026 make accurate mass balance and country traceability increasingly important.
- Representative sampling and chain of custody are as important as analytical precision.

## Chapter Summary

Honey quality is best managed as an evidence system rather than a list of numbers. Moisture, sugars, acidity, conductivity, enzymes, HMF, sensory characteristics, residues, pollen, and authenticity tests each answer different questions. Correct interpretation requires knowing the sample, floral context, processing history, and market standard.

Current Codex and EU standards provide core compositional criteria. In the EU, ordinary honey generally has a maximum moisture of 20%, a general free-acidity maximum of 50 meq/kg, conductivity criteria around 0.8 mS/cm that distinguish broad honey categories with exceptions, a general diastase minimum of 8 Schade units, and a general HMF maximum of 40 mg/kg with defined exceptions. Sugar criteria also vary by honey type and named botanical sources.

Authenticity requires a broader toolkit. Pollen analysis can support botanical and geographical origin, while stable isotopes, NMR, chromatography, mass spectrometry, DNA approaches, and other validated techniques can investigate different kinds of fraud. The strongest conclusions combine laboratory evidence with traceability and mass balance.

Finally, sampling controls the value of every result. A precise laboratory number from an unrepresentative or mislabelled sample can mislead more than a modest field measurement taken correctly. Good honey quality therefore begins at the hive, continues through processing and storage, and ends with documented evidence that the product matches its identity and specification.
