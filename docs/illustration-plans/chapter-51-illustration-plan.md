## Planned Figures

### Figure 51.1 — What Changes During Storage
Central sealed honey container with branches to HMF increase, enzyme decline, aroma loss, colour change, moisture exchange, crystallisation and possible fermentation.

### Figure 51.2 — Food Safety, Quality, and Shelf Life
Three overlapping circles showing safety hazards, quality specifications and the time period during which the packaged product meets its defined specification.

### Figure 51.3 — Hygroscopicity and Sealing
Open versus sealed container in humid air, showing moisture movement into exposed honey and protection provided by a sound closure.

### Figure 51.4 — Headspace and Condensation
Tank cross-section during temperature change showing humid headspace air condensing on a cold lid and droplets falling toward the honey. Include mitigation through stable temperature and closed-container handling.

### Figure 51.5 — Time–Temperature Storage Effect
Conceptual curves showing faster HMF increase and enzyme/aroma loss at warmer storage conditions. Do not provide a universal shelf-life conversion.

### Figure 51.6 — Crystallisation and Phase Separation
Sequence: liquid honey → fine/normal crystallisation → partial phase separation with crystal-rich lower layer and relatively wetter liquid upper layer. Highlight fermentation risk in the liquid phase.

### Figure 51.7 — Fermentation Versus Surface Foam
Side-by-side panels distinguishing temporary trapped-air/wax foam from continuing gas, pressure and off-odour associated with fermentation.

### Figure 51.8 — Bulk Container Options
Food-grade stainless tank, food-grade drum, HDPE bucket/drum and food-certified IBC, with key checks: previous use, seal, food compatibility, valve hygiene and structural condition.

### Figure 51.9 — Retail Packaging Trade-Offs
Glass versus food-grade plastic: barrier, breakage, light, weight, heat sensitivity and closure integrity.

### Figure 51.10 — Warehouse Layout
Dry food-storage area with coded locations, clean pallets, chemical segregation, quarantine zone, pest control, temperature monitoring and safe forklift aisles.

### Figure 51.11 — FIFO/FEFO and Harvest Age
Lot cards moving through storage, demonstrating why internal harvest/source age remains important even when products are packed later.

### Figure 51.12 — Storage Sampling
Tank and drum sampling diagrams showing homogenisation considerations, clean sample points, separate container IDs and the danger of sampling only a separated top layer.

### Figure 51.13 — Quarantine Status System
HOLD / RELEASED / REJECTED / REWORK ELIGIBLE examples with physical segregation and unique lot identity.

### Figure 51.14 — Storage Emergency Responses
Leak, bulging container, missing label, chemical exposure and flood event, all leading to segregation and investigation rather than automatic release.

## Accuracy Requirements

- Do not portray crystallisation as spoilage or adulteration.
- Phase-separation artwork must show moisture redistribution conceptually without inventing fixed percentages.
- No universal storage temperature or shelf life may be presented as correct for all honey types.
- Cold-storage diagrams must include condensation risk during warm/humid transition.
- Unknown lots must be shown as quarantined, not identified by colour.
- Food-contact containers must never be represented by reused chemical drums.

## Accessibility

- Use labels, icons and patterns in addition to colour.
- Graphs must remain legible in greyscale.
- Emergency-status figures must not rely on red/green colour alone.
