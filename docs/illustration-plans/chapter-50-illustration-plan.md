## Planned Figures

### Figure 50.1 — Minimal-Processing Flow
Extracted lot → quality check → settling → appropriate straining → controlled warming only if needed → documented blending/homogenisation if intended → identified storage/packing vessel.

### Figure 50.2 — Settling Tank Separation
Tank cutaway showing floating foam/fine wax, clear honey zone and denser sediment, with a covered lid and sampling/outlet points.

### Figure 50.3 — Straining Versus Legal “Filtered Honey”
Coarse debris-removal mesh versus a process causing significant pollen removal. Caption must clearly separate ordinary beekeeping terminology from jurisdiction-specific legal product definitions.

### Figure 50.4 — Time–Temperature Quality Relationship
Conceptual chart showing increasing thermal exposure associated with higher HMF and declining enzyme/aroma preservation. No universal process recipe should be embedded.

### Figure 50.5 — Uniform Versus Localised Heating
Jacketed tank/gentle warm room versus direct hot surface producing an overheated boundary layer.

### Figure 50.6 — Drum Liquefaction Temperature Gradient
Bulk drum with warmer outer honey and colder/crystallised core to show why heater setpoint does not equal product temperature.

### Figure 50.7 — Gentle Versus Turbulent Mixing
Slow whole-tank circulation without vortex compared with high-speed mixing that entrains air while leaving dead zones.

### Figure 50.8 — Blend Traceability
Source lots with weights/origins/quality data → blending tank → new blend lot → retained records supporting final origin percentages.

### Figure 50.9 — Crystallisation Factors
Glucose/water ratio, temperature, nuclei/pollen, seed crystals and storage history feeding into nucleation, crystal size and texture.

### Figure 50.10 — Moisture-Management Options
Controlled dry-air conditioning of comb, thin-film/vacuum bulk technologies and a prominent jurisdiction/legal-validation checkpoint. Do not imply every technique is permitted everywhere.

### Figure 50.11 — Honey Processing Hazard Map
Biological, chemical and physical hazards mapped to incoming lots, pump seals, cleaning chemicals, glass/metal, water ingress and pests.

### Figure 50.12 — HMF and Diastase as Quality Evidence
Conceptual paired indicators: HMF tends to increase with heat/storage while enzyme activity can decline; both also depend on natural honey characteristics and history.

### Figure 50.13 — Fraud-Prevention Controls
Approved supplier → traceable lot → mass balance → laboratory authenticity testing where risk requires → documented blend → label/origin verification.

### Figure 50.14 — Unsupported Home “Purity Tests”
Crossed-out examples of flame, paper, thumb and water-spread tests with caption: physical behaviour is not reliable proof of honey authenticity.

## Accuracy Requirements

- Do not portray a legal HMF maximum as a desirable processing target.
- Do not imply one diastase value can reconstruct exact heating history.
- No figure may show adding water, syrup or undeclared ingredients to honey.
- Pollen must not be portrayed simply as dirt.
- Moisture-reduction technologies must include legal/product-identity caution.
- Fraud testing must be shown as laboratory/reference-data based, not home folklore.
- Honey must not be described as sterile.

## Accessibility

- Use labels, arrows and patterns in addition to colour.
- Time–temperature and quality charts must remain legible in greyscale.
- Captions should distinguish product-quality optimisation from legal compliance.
